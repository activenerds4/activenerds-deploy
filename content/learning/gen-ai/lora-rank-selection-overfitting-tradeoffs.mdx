## Question
When choosing a LoRA rank (r) and target modules for a fine-tuning job, what is the practical trade-off between rank size, adapter expressiveness, and overfitting risk on a small domain-specific dataset?


## Quick Answer
Rank `r` sets the ceiling on how expressive a LoRA adapter's update can be — too low and the adapter can't represent the behavior change you're trying to teach; too high and, especially on a small domain-specific dataset, the adapter has enough capacity to memorize the training set rather than generalize from it.

## Detailed Answer
Rank directly controls the size of the subspace the adapter is allowed to move the model within (see how `A` and `B` compose into `ΔW`): a higher `r` means a larger, more expressive space of possible weight updates, at the direct cost of more trainable parameters and a weaker implicit regularization effect.

| Rank range | Expressiveness | Typical fit | Overfitting risk on small datasets |
|------------|-----------------|--------------|--------------------------------------|
| 4–8 | Low — narrow, constrained update | Light stylistic or tone adjustments, small datasets (hundreds to low thousands of examples) | Low — strong regularization from the tiny subspace |
| 16–32 | Moderate | Most single-domain fine-tuning tasks (support-ticket classification, domain Q&A) | Moderate — reasonable default for datasets in the tens of thousands |
| 64+ | High — approaches full fine-tuning's flexibility | Substantial behavior or reasoning shifts, large and diverse datasets | High on small datasets — enough capacity to memorize rather than generalize |

The practical failure mode at high rank with a small dataset looks exactly like classic overfitting: training loss keeps dropping while validation performance on held-out examples stalls or degrades, because the adapter has enough capacity to fit noise and idiosyncrasies specific to the training examples rather than the underlying pattern behind them. This is the same overfitting dynamic full fine-tuning is prone to — high rank is, in the limit, just full fine-tuning with extra steps, so it inherits more of that risk the closer it gets.

**Target modules matter alongside rank.** Applying LoRA to more of the network (query/key/value/output projections *and* the MLP layers, rather than just attention projections) increases total adapter capacity in the same way raising `r` does — it's a second knob controlling the same trade-off, and the two compound. A common practical approach is to start conservative (rank 8–16, attention-only) on a small dataset, and only increase rank or broaden target modules if validation metrics show the model is genuinely underfitting the target behavior, not just slow to converge.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Key Takeaways">
Match rank to dataset size and task complexity, not to "as high as memory allows" — on a small domain-specific dataset, a lower rank that regularizes hard usually generalizes better than a high rank that has room to memorize the training set.
</Callout>

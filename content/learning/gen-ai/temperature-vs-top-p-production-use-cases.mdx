## Question
From a systems perspective, what is actually happening to the model's output probability distribution when you lower temperature versus when you apply top-p (nucleus) sampling — and why would you choose one lever over the other for a production use case like code generation vs. creative writing?


## Quick Answer
Lowering **temperature** reshapes the entire probability distribution before any cutoff is applied — sharpening it so the model's already-favored tokens dominate even more — while **top-p** leaves the underlying distribution's shape untouched and instead trims away the low-probability tail *after* the distribution is computed, so the two levers act on different parts of the pipeline and aren't interchangeable.

## Detailed Answer
The generation pipeline runs in a fixed order: the model outputs raw logits, temperature rescales those logits before softmax, softmax converts them to a probability distribution, and then a cutoff rule like top-p decides which of those probabilities are even eligible to be sampled.

**Temperature** operates at the first stage. Dividing logits by $T < 1$ before softmax exaggerates the gap between high- and low-probability tokens, concentrating probability mass onto whatever the model already found most likely — it doesn't remove any tokens from consideration, it just makes the winners win by more. Pushed to the extreme ($T \to 0$), this converges to greedy, fully deterministic decoding.

**Top-P (nucleus sampling)** operates at the last stage, after the (possibly temperature-adjusted) distribution already exists. It sorts tokens by probability, keeps adding them until their cumulative mass crosses threshold $P$, and hard-excludes everything else — so it directly controls how many distinct candidates are even in play, adapting turn to turn based on how confident or uncertain the underlying distribution happens to be at that step.

This distinction is exactly why production systems reach for different levers depending on the task:

| Use case | Preferred lever | Why |
|-----------|------------------|------|
| Code generation, structured output, tool calls | Low temperature (near 0), narrow or no top-p | Syntax correctness has essentially one right answer at each step; sharpening toward the model's most confident token minimizes the risk of a syntax-breaking outlier, and top-p adds little once the distribution is already this peaked |
| General-purpose chat, factual Q&A | Moderate temperature (~0.5–0.8) with moderate top-p (~0.9) | Wants some natural variation in phrasing without drifting into implausible or incoherent continuations |
| Creative writing, brainstorming | Higher temperature (~0.9–1.2) with a wider top-p (~0.95) | Rewards surprising, less-obvious word choices; top-p still guards against genuinely nonsensical low-probability tokens sneaking in even as temperature widens the field |

For code generation specifically, the risk profile is asymmetric: an unusual but grammatically valid word choice in a story is harmless, but an unusual token in the middle of a function signature can break the build entirely — which is why code-focused pipelines lean almost entirely on temperature reduction rather than top-p, since top-p alone doesn't guarantee the *most* likely token wins, only that the tail is excluded.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Key Takeaways">
Temperature decides how confidently the model commits to its favorites, while top-p decides how large the pool of acceptable alternatives is — production systems tune the one that matches whether the task has a narrow correct answer or a wide space of acceptable ones.
</Callout>

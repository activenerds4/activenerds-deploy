## Question
Why do most modern diffusion image models (e.g., Stable Diffusion-style architectures) perform the denoising process in a compressed latent space rather than directly on pixels, and what's the engineering trade-off being made?


## Quick Answer
Running the iterative denoising process directly on full-resolution pixels is prohibitively expensive, so **latent diffusion** models first compress an image into a much smaller learned representation, run the entire multi-step denoising loop there, and only decompress back to pixels once at the very end — trading a small amount of fine-detail fidelity for a large reduction in compute and memory cost.

## Detailed Answer
Denoising is not a single pass — it's a loop that runs the model's forward pass dozens of times per image, and each of those passes involves attention and convolution operations whose cost scales with the size of whatever representation is being processed. Running that entire loop directly on, say, a 1024×1024-pixel image at full resolution multiplies an already-expensive operation by a huge spatial dimension, at every one of the many denoising steps — quickly becoming impractical for anything beyond a research setting with substantial compute.

**Latent diffusion** solves this with a separate, pre-trained **autoencoder**: an encoder network compresses a full-resolution image down into a much smaller **latent representation** (commonly shrinking each spatial dimension by a factor of 8, so a 512×512 image becomes something like a 64×64 latent grid with more channels), and a matching decoder can reconstruct a full-resolution image back from that latent. Crucially, this autoencoder is trained separately, ahead of time, specifically to preserve the perceptually important structure of images through compression and reconstruction — it's optimized so that round-tripping through the latent space loses as little visually meaningful information as possible.

With that compressed representation available, the entire expensive part — the forward diffusion training and the iterative reverse denoising at inference — happens entirely within this much smaller latent space, rather than on raw pixels. Only at the very end of inference does the final denoised latent get passed through the decoder once, converting it back into a full-resolution pixel image. Since the latent space might be 1/64th the spatial size of the original image (an 8x reduction per dimension, squared), the compute and memory savings across dozens of denoising steps are substantial — this is a major part of why models like Stable Diffusion could run on consumer GPUs at all.

The trade-off is real, not free: any information the autoencoder failed to preserve during compression is permanently unavailable to the denoising process, since the diffusion model never sees the original pixels directly. This shows up as some loss of very fine detail or texture fidelity compared to a hypothetical pixel-space diffusion model, and it means the overall system's output quality is capped by how good the pre-trained autoencoder's compression is — a weak autoencoder bottlenecks the whole pipeline regardless of how good the diffusion model itself is.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Key Takeaways">
Latent diffusion isn't a shortcut around the denoising process — it's a deliberate architectural split that moves the expensive iterative work into a small, information-dense space and accepts a bounded compression loss in exchange for making the whole approach computationally practical.
</Callout>

Most alerting is noise because most alerting isn't tied to what users actually experience. A threshold on CPU usage fires at 80% whether or not any user is affected; a latency alert fires on a percentile that may not correspond to any real user's experience. SLOs — Service Level Objectives — fix this by starting from the user and working backward to the metrics, so every alert corresponds to a real reliability loss that a real user felt. The side effect, which is why SLOs matter for engineer wellbeing as much as for reliability, is that they give you a principled way to turn off the alerts that don't matter.

This guide covers why SLOs, choosing the indicators that matter, setting the objective, and operating with error budgets without burning out the team.

## Why SLOs

The premise of SLOs is that reliability is a product feature with a cost, not a binary "it works." A service that's 100% available is either trivial or over-engineered — the cost of the last few nines of reliability is exponential, and almost no product justifies it. The question is not "is it reliable" but "is it reliable enough for what users expect, and are we spending appropriately to get there."

**Error budgets as a throttle.** An SLO of 99.9% availability gives you an error budget: 0.1% of the time, the service can be unavailable, and you're still meeting the objective. The budget is the permission to take risks — to ship changes that might cause brief regressions, to do risky migrations, to learn from failures — without the org treating every incident as a moral failure. A team with no error budget ships cautiously and slowly; a team with a healthy budget ships aggressively and learns faster. The budget is the mechanism that balances reliability against velocity, which is the actual tension in any platform team.

**Reducing alert noise.** This is the payoff for engineer burnout. SLO-based alerting fires when the error budget is burning too fast — when the rate of errors or latency violations threatens to exhaust the budget before the window resets — not on every individual blip. A CPU spike that doesn't affect users produces no alert; a latency regression that does affect users alerts based on its impact on the budget. The result is fewer, more meaningful alerts, which is the single biggest improvement to on-call quality of life. The [on-call sustainability guide](/blog/sustainable-pacing-oncall-rotations) covers the human side; SLOs are the technical lever that makes it possible.

## Choosing SLIs

The SLI (Service Level Indicator) is the measurement; the SLO is the target you set on it. Choosing the SLI well is most of the work, because a bad SLI produces a bad SLO no matter how carefully you set the target.

**User-centric metrics.** The SLI should measure something a user experiences, not something the infrastructure does. "HTTP 200 rate" is borderline — it's user-facing but doesn't capture whether the response was fast enough to be useful. "Percentage of requests served under 500ms" is user-centric — it measures the experience. "CPU utilization" is infrastructure-centric and should never be an SLI; it's a cause of bad user experience, not the experience itself. The test: if a user couldn't tell whether this metric got better or worse, it's not an SLI.

**Latency and availability.** The two most common SLI types, and usually both are needed. Availability is the error rate (failed requests / total). Latency is the distribution of response times, typically measured at a percentile (the proportion of requests under a threshold). The percentile matters — a 95th-percentile latency target tolerates 5% of requests being slow; a 99th-percentile target is stricter. Most services need both an availability SLO and a latency SLO, because a service that's available but slow is a different failure than one that's fast but erroring.

**Avoiding vanity metrics.** Metrics that look good but don't reflect user experience are vanity metrics. Uptime as measured by "the server is running" is a vanity metric — the server can be running and returning errors. Request volume is a vanity metric for reliability (it's a capacity metric). The trap is that vanity metrics are easy to measure and easy to game, so they proliferate in dashboards. An SLI review should ask of each metric: "does this measure what the user experiences, or what the system does?"

## Setting the Objective

The SLO is the target on the SLI, and setting it well is a calibration exercise, not an aspiration exercise.

**Calibrating with history.** The SLO should reflect what's achievable and what users need, not what you wish were true. Look at historical performance: if your service has been 99.5% available over the last quarter, an SLO of 99.99% is fiction — you'll burn the budget constantly and the team will learn to ignore the alerts. An SLO of 99.5% (matching reality) or 99.7% (a slight stretch) is honest and actionable. The SLO is a description of the reliability you commit to and can sustain, calibrated against what you've actually delivered.

**Window-based budgets.** The error budget is computed over a window — typically 30 days. A 99.9% SLO over 30 days gives ~43 minutes of allowed unavailability. The window length is a tradeoff: a short window (e.g., 7 days) reacts fast but is jittery; a long window (e.g., 90 days) is stable but slow to surface regressions. 30 days is a common middle ground. The budget should be visible to the team — a burn-down chart of remaining budget is the operational view that makes the SLO usable day-to-day.

**Rolling vs calendar windows.** A rolling window (last 30 days from now) is continuous and smooths over calendar boundaries. A calendar window (the current month) resets on a fixed schedule, which is easier to reason about ("we have 20 minutes of budget left this month") but creates cliff effects at the reset. Rolling is generally better for operations; calendar is better for reporting. Many teams use rolling for the operational SLO and calendar for the reporting view.

## Operating with SLOs

The SLO is only useful if it drives operational behavior, which means alerting and decision-making tied to the budget.

**Burn rate alerting.** The alerting strategy that makes SLOs livable: alert based on how fast the error budget is burning, not on individual events. A "fast burn" alert (budget burning at a rate that would exhaust it in less than X hours) pages immediately; a "slow burn" alert (budget burning at a rate that would exhaust it before the window resets) creates a ticket. This means a brief outage pages if it's severe enough, a slow degradation creates a tracked issue, and a single error doesn't alert at all. The result is alerts that correspond to real reliability threats, which is what reduces the noise. Multi-window burn rate (checking the burn rate over both a short and long window) avoids both alerting on transient blips and missing slow burns.

**Budget spent decisions.** The error budget should drive decisions, not just alerts. When the budget is healthy, the team ships aggressively — risky changes, migrations, experiments — because the budget exists to be spent on learning. When the budget is exhausted or nearly so, the team shifts to stabilization — freeze risky changes, focus on reliability work, address the causes of the burn. This is the mechanism that makes SLOs a velocity tool, not just a reliability tool: the budget explicitly funds risk-taking when there's room and constrains it when there isn't. Without this decision rule, the SLO is just a dashboard.

**Reviewing and adjusting.** SLOs aren't set once. Review them quarterly: is the SLO still right for what users need? Is the team consistently under or over budget, and what does that tell you about the calibration? A team that's always under budget with no alerts may have set the SLO too loosely (and is over-spending on reliability); a team that's always over budget may have set it too tightly (or has real reliability work to do). The review is where the SLO stays honest — without it, the SLO drifts into fiction and the team learns to ignore it again.

The honest framing: SLOs are an investment that pays off in two ways — better reliability decisions, because you're measuring what users actually experience, and better on-call life, because the alerts that fire are the ones that matter. The upfront cost is real (defining SLIs, calibrating, building the burn-rate alerting), and it's a multi-week project, not an afternoon. But the alternative — alerting on infrastructure metrics that don't map to user experience, paging on every blip, and burning out the on-call rotation — is more expensive in the long run, in both reliability and people. SLOs are how you make reliability a deliberate, sustainable practice rather than a constant, exhausting reaction.

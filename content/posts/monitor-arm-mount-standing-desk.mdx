A [standing desk](/articles/standing-desk-mat-comparison) without a monitor arm is a half-finished setup. When you sit, your monitor needs to be lower. When you stand, it needs to rise — often by 10-14 inches.

A static [monitor stand](/articles/ergonomic-monitor-stand-guide) can't do both. You'll end up craning your neck in one position, which is the exact problem a [standing desk](/articles/standing-desk-converter-comparison) was supposed to solve. A monitor arm is the cheapest high-impact ergonomic upgrade you can make.

## Why a Monitor Arm Matters

### Height Adjustability for Sit-Stand

The core benefit: your monitor moves with you. A good arm lets you reposition your screen in seconds whether sitting or standing, keeping the top of the monitor at or slightly below eye level in both positions. Static stands force a compromise that always loses.

### Desk Space Recovery

A monitor base eats 8-12 inches of desk depth. An arm clamps to the edge and lifts the monitor off the surface, freeing that space for a keyboard, notebook, or (ideally) nothing — negative space reduces visual clutter and cognitive load.

### Ergonomic Positioning

Beyond height, an arm lets you adjust:
- **Depth**: pull the monitor closer or push it back to reduce eye strain
- **Tilt**: angle the screen to reduce glare
- **Rotation**: switch to portrait for code review or long documents
- **Lateral shift**: move the monitor off-center to match your seated position

### Stability at Full Height

The biggest complaint with cheap arms is wobble at full extension — the monitor jiggles when you type or adjust the desk. The Ergotron LX and Humanscale M/Flex both use heavier-gauge steel and tighter joints that minimize this. If your monitor shakes when you type, the arm is the problem, not your typing.

### What You Compromise On

Budget arms share three weaknesses:
1. **Tension drift**: the monitor slowly sinks over hours, requiring re-adjustment
2. **Wobble at extension**: more flex in the arm at full reach
3. **Adjustment friction**: changing height requires more force, sometimes tools

If you adjust your monitor multiple times daily (typical for sit-stand users), these add up. If you set it once, they don't matter.

## Dual Monitor Solutions

### Side-by-Side Arms

Two single arms (one per monitor) give the most flexibility — each monitor moves independently. This is ideal if you use one monitor as a primary focus screen and the other as a secondary reference display at a different height.

**Cost:** Two Ergotron LX arms = ~$340

### Pole-Mounted Options

A single pole with two arms mounted to it keeps both monitors aligned vertically and saves desk clamp space. The Humanscale M/Flex and Ergotron HX are examples.

**Cost:** $350-$500

### Weight Capacity Matters

Always check the arm's weight rating against your monitor's actual weight (not the shipping weight). A 34-inch ultrawide can weigh 18-22 lbs without a stand — right at the limit of a 20-lb arm. Add a monitor light bar and you may exceed it. When in doubt, buy the heavier-duty arm.

## Installation Tips

### Desk Clamp vs Grommet

- **Desk clamp**: attaches to the desk edge. Works on most standing desks. Requires 1-2 inches of clear desk edge.
- **Grommet mount**: passes through a hole in the desk. More stable, but requires drilling (or a pre-existing grommet hole).

Most standing desks come with a grommet hole — use it. The mount is more stable and doesn't eat edge space.

### Cable Management

Cable management is what separates a clean setup from a rat's nest. Run power and display cables through the arm's built-in channels (premium arms have them; budget arms often don't). Use velcro wraps, not zip ties — you'll re-route cables when you add or remove displays.

<Callout type="tip">
**Pro tip:** Use right-angle display adapters where cables meet the monitor. Standard straight cables stick out 3-4 inches and limit how close the monitor can sit to the arm. Right-angle adapters let the monitor tuck in fully.
</Callout>

### Tension Adjustment

Every monitor arm needs tension calibration for your specific monitor weight. The process:
1. Mount the monitor
2. Loosen the tension screw until the monitor slowly drifts down
3. Tighten until it holds steady
4. Test by moving it up and down — it should stay where you leave it

Skip this step and your monitor will either drift or require excessive force to move. Budget arms need this more often; premium arms hold calibration longer.

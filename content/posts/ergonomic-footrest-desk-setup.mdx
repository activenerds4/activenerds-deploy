The footrest is the most over-prescribed and under-understood piece of [ergonomic gear](/articles/best-ergonomic-gear-programmers). Walk into any office store and you'll find a wall of them, marketed as essential for "proper posture." The reality is narrower: a footrest solves a specific problem (your chair is too high for your legs to reach the floor comfortably), and if you don't have that problem, a footrest doesn't help and may even hurt. It's a compensation for a setup mismatch, not a universal good.

This is the practical take — what a footrest actually does, when you need one, and when fixing the chair is the better answer.

## What a Footrest Actually Does

A footrest does one thing: it gives your feet something to rest on when they can't reach the floor. That's it. The benefit, when it applies, is that it relieves pressure on the backs of your thighs. When your chair is too high and your feet dangle or you perch on the edge, your thighs press into the seat, which compresses the blood vessels and nerves there, leading to the numbness and discomfort that builds over a long sit. A footrest lets you sit back in the chair (using the backrest) while keeping your feet supported, which removes the thigh pressure.

For shorter users, this is the core use case. If the desk height is set for an average or taller person (which most shared desks are), a shorter person has to raise their chair to reach the keyboard at the right height, which lifts their feet off the floor. The footrest is the fix: raise the chair to the right desk height, support the feet with the rest. Without it, the shorter user either strains their shoulders (desk too high) or dangles their feet (chair too high) — both bad. With it, both are solved.

The other legitimate use is a fixed-height desk or chair you can't adjust. If your desk is a fixed counter height or your chair is a fixed stool, and you can't change either, a footrest is the only lever you have. It's a workaround for a constraint you can't remove, and in that situation it's genuinely useful. But notice what these use cases have in common: the footrest is compensating for a setup that can't be made right directly. That's the actual role of a footrest.

## When You Need One

The signs are specific. **Chair too high**: your feet don't rest flat on the floor when your chair is at the right height for the desk. You find yourself either perching on the edge (to get feet down) or sitting back with feet dangling.

**Thigh pressure**: the backs of your thighs feel compressed or numb after sitting, which eases when you sit forward. **Short stature relative to the desk**: you've raised the chair to reach the keyboard and now your feet are off the floor. Each of these is the footrest's problem to solve.

When you don't need one: if your chair is adjustable and your feet rest flat on the floor with your knees at roughly 90 degrees and your thighs parallel to the floor, a footrest is unnecessary and may push your posture out of alignment. Adding a footrest to a setup that's already correct raises your feet, which changes your hip angle, which can tilt your pelvis and create the back discomfort the footrest was supposed to prevent. More gear isn't more ergonomic; correct gear is. If the setup is right, leave it alone.

The standing-desk use case is a separate one: some people use a footrest to prop one foot up while standing (a "standing footrest" or bar), which shifts weight and lets one hip extend, countering the hip-flexion of standing. This is a real and different use case — it's about varying standing posture, not compensating for a too-high chair — and it's worth considering if you stand a lot, but it's not the same product as a sitting footrest.

## Choosing One

If you've determined you need one, the features that matter:

**Adjustable angle.** The single most important feature. A fixed-angle footrest assumes one leg length and one chair height, which is almost never exactly right. An adjustable-angle rest (typically 0-30 degrees) lets you set the height to match your setup, which is the whole point. Skip the fixed ones; they're cheap but they're a guess at your dimensions.

**Non-slip surface.** Your feet will shift on it over a long sit, and a slippery surface (hard plastic) means your feet slide off, defeating the purpose. A textured or rubberized surface, or a fabric cover, keeps feet put. This sounds minor and isn't — a footrest you're constantly repositioning is a footrest you stop using.

**Size.** It needs to be wide enough for both feet comfortably (you'll shift position), and deep enough that your feet rest on it without hanging off. Too small and you're constantly adjusting; too large and it's underfoot clutter. Roughly 40-45cm wide and 30cm deep covers most people. The height-adjustable ones (that raise the whole platform, not just the angle) are worth it if your chair is significantly too high.

The features that don't matter much: massage bumps and texture patterns (marketing), built-in rockers (a gimmick for most), and premium materials (a functional footrest is a functional footrest). Don't overspend; a $40 adjustable, non-slip rest does the job as well as a $150 one with bells on.

## Alternatives and Verdict

**Fixing the chair first.** Before buying a footrest, ask whether you can fix the root cause. If your chair is adjustable, lower it until your feet are flat — and if that makes the desk too high, the problem is the desk, not the chair, and a footrest is compensating for a desk that's too high (consider a keyboard tray to lower the typing surface instead). The footrest is the right answer when the chair can't be lowered (desk too high, can't change it) or the desk can't be lowered (fixed height). When you can fix the root cause, fixing it beats adding a compensating accessory — the [ergonomic desk setup](/blog/ergonomic-desk-setup-programmers) is about getting the fundamentals right, and a footrest is a fallback, not a fundamental.

**DIY options.** A box, a stack of books, a folded yoga mat — anything that's the right height and stable works. The footrest doesn't need to be a specialized product; it needs to be the right height and not slide. If you're not sure you need one, try a DIY version for a week before buying. If it helps, you've confirmed the need and can buy a nicer one; if it doesn't, you've saved the money and learned the footrest wasn't your problem.

** A footrest is worth it if you have the too-high-chair problem and can't fix it directly. It's not worth it as a general "ergonomic upgrade" to an already-correct setup. The marketing implies everyone needs one; the reality is a fraction of users, mostly shorter users at fixed-height desks.

If that's you, a $40 adjustable rest is a small investment that solves a real discomfort. If it's not you, the money is better spent on the [chair](/blog/ergonomic-chair-buying-guide-programmers) or the desk, which are the things that actually determine your posture. The footrest is a tool for a specific problem, and like any tool, it's good when it fits the problem and irrelevant when it doesn't.

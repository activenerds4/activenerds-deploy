"Multitasking" is one of those words that means two different things, and the difference is the whole argument. In the popular sense, multitasking is doing several things at once and getting more done. In the cognitive science sense, multitasking is rapidly switching attention between tasks, and it has a measurable cost on each switch. The popular sense is a myth — the brain doesn't do two demanding things simultaneously; it switches, and the switching isn't free. For engineers, whose work is mostly the demanding kind, the cost is real and the structure of the day should account for it.

This is the evidence-based case for single-tasking, what the [switching cost](/articles/managing-context-switching-engineering) actually is, and how to structure a day around it without pretending you can eliminate all switching.

## The Multitasking Myth

What "multitasking" really is, for cognitive work, is task-switching. You're not writing code and reviewing a PR at the same time; you're writing code, then switching to review, then switching back. Each switch has a cost: the time to re-load the context of the task you're switching to. For a coding task, that context is the problem you're solving, the file you're in, the variable names, the approach you were trying, where you left off. Reloading it takes minutes, not seconds, and it has to happen every time you switch back.

The switching cost studies are consistent and clear: when people switch between tasks, they're slower and make more errors than when they do the same tasks sequentially, and the effect is larger for complex tasks and longer interruptions. The oft-cited "it takes 23 minutes to refocus after an interruption" is a specific study with specific conditions, but the direction is robust: interruptions cost more than the time of the interruption itself, because of the re-loading. For a developer deep in a debugging session who gets pulled into a 5-minute question, the cost isn't 5 minutes — it's 5 minutes plus the time to rebuild the mental model of the bug, which can be most of an hour if the model was complex.

Why it feels productive is the trap. Switching feels productive because each switch is a small [dopamine](/articles/dopamine-detox-developers) event (a new thing to attend to, a fresh start), and because the switching itself is busy and busy feels productive. This is the same mechanism that makes [context switching](/blog/managing-context-switching-engineering) feel productive while destroying output: the feeling of productivity and the reality of productivity diverge, and the feeling is what people optimize for. The multitasker feels they're getting more done; the measurement shows they're getting less, more slowly, with more errors.

## The Real Cost of Switching

Three components of the cost, each worth understanding because each suggests a different mitigation.

**Attention residue.** When you switch from task A to task B, part of your attention stays on A — you're still thinking about the problem you left, the email you didn't finish, the question you didn't answer. This residue reduces the cognitive capacity available for B, which is why the first few minutes on a new task feel foggy even though you've "switched." The residue clears over time, but if you switch frequently, you're always operating with residue from the last task, which is a chronic tax on the current one. This is why people who switch a lot describe feeling "scattered" — they literally are, attentionally.

**Ramp-up time.** The time to re-load the task's context. For shallow tasks (reply to an email), this is short; for deep tasks (debug a distributed system), it's long, because the context is large. The cost scales with the depth of the task, which is why switching is most expensive for exactly the work that's most valuable — the deep technical work that requires holding a large mental model. A day of [deep work](/articles/deep-work-techniques-engineers) interrupted by frequent switches can spend more time ramping up than actually working, which is why such days feel busy and produce little.

**Error rates.** Switching increases errors, particularly in the period right after a switch when attention is split. The errors are subtle — a wrong variable, a missed edge case, a test you forgot to run — and they're costly because they're discovered later, when they're harder to fix. The error cost is often invisible (you don't see the bug you didn't write), but it shows up in the review and the production incidents that trace back to "I was in a rush." Reducing switching reduces these errors, which is a quiet but large productivity gain.

## When Switching Is Unavoidable

The realistic version isn't "never switch" — it's "switch deliberately, not reflexively." Some switching is unavoidable and some is chosen, and the goal is to minimize the cost, not pretend it's zero.

**On-call reality.** On-call is enforced switching — a page pulls you out of whatever you're doing, and you switch to the incident. This is unavoidable, and the mitigation is to not schedule deep work during on-call, because you know it'll be interrupted. Plan on-call days for shallow work (reviews, planning, docs) that switches cheaply, and protect non-on-call days for deep work. Don't fight the constraint; structure around it.

**Batching similar work.** When you do have to switch, batch the switches. Instead of checking email every 20 minutes (a switch each time, each with ramp-up), check it three times a day — you switch three times instead of twenty, and the total ramp-up cost drops dramatically. The same for code reviews, Slack messages, and other shallow work: batch them into defined windows, and protect the windows between for deep work. Batching is the single most effective switching-cost reduction, because it converts many small switches into a few larger ones.

**Protected blocks.** The deepest mitigation is time-blocking: a defined block (90 minutes is a good length, matching a natural focus cycle) during which you don't switch. No email, no Slack, no reviews — one task, for the block. The block isn't about willpower; it's about removing the triggers that cause the reflexive switches. Close the tabs, silence the notifications, put the phone away, and the switching doesn't happen because the cues are gone. The [deep work techniques](/blog/deep-work-techniques-engineers) guide covers the mechanics; the principle is that a protected block makes single-tasking the default rather than a discipline feat.

## Designing a Single-Tasking Day

The structure that makes single-tasking the path of least resistance, rather than a constant effort of will.

**One thing at a time.** The day is a sequence of single-tasking blocks, not a parallel juggle. A morning deep-work block on one task, a midday batch of shallow work (email, reviews, messages) in one window, an afternoon block on a second task. Each block is one thing; the transitions between blocks are the deliberate switches, not the reflexive ones inside a block. The day has fewer switches, and each switch is planned.

**Visible task queues.** When a thought about another task arises during a block ("I should reply to that email," "I need to check that build"), don't switch — write it down and keep going. A visible queue (a notepad, a todo list) captures the interrupting thought without acting on it, which lets you stay on the current task without the fear of forgetting the other thing. The queue is the tool that makes single-tasking sustainable — it handles the part of your brain that wants to switch by giving it a place to put the thought, so the thought doesn't pull you off task.

**Handling interruptions.** Real interruptions (a colleague with a question, an on-call page) happen; the goal is to handle them with minimal switching cost. For a quick question, answer it and return (the cost is the ramp-up back to your task, which the visible queue helps with — you wrote down where you were). For a longer interruption, either commit to it fully (switch) or defer it ("can I come back to this in an hour?"). The worst option is the half-switch — trying to keep one foot in the original task while handling the interruption — which leaves attention residue on both and does neither well.

The honest framing: single-tasking isn't a moral virtue or a productivity hack; it's the recognition that cognitive work has a switching cost and that the cost is large for the deep work engineers do most. The day that accounts for this — batched shallow work, protected deep blocks, a visible queue for interrupting thoughts — gets more real work done than the day that juggles, and with less stress. Pair it with the [context-switching management](/blog/managing-context-switching-engineering) practices and an honest look at the [dopamine-driven](/blog/dopamine-detox-developers) reasons switching feels good, and single-tasking becomes the structure of the day rather than a struggle against it.

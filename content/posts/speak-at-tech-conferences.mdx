Speaking at a [tech conference](/articles/tech-conference-networking-strategies) is the highest-leverage career move most engineers never make. One good talk gets you on the radar of hiring managers, open-source maintainers, and potential collaborators in a way that years of Slack lurking can't. But the path from "I've never spoken" to "I'm on stage at KubeCon" is opaque. This is the step-by-step guide I wish I'd had for my first talk.

## Finding the Right CFP

### Where to Find CFPs

A CFP (Call for Proposals) is the open application process most conferences use to select speakers. The main aggregators:

- **cfp.land**: the largest aggregator, filterable by topic and location
- **Papercall.io**: focused on tech conferences, shows submission counts
- **devevents.com**: community-maintained calendar
- **Conference websites directly**: the most reliable for major events (KubeCon, QCon, GTC)

Set up alerts for your topics (Kubernetes, ML, security) so you catch CFPs when they open, not when they close.

### Matching Your Expertise

The most common mistake first-timers make is proposing a topic they think is impressive rather than one they actually know. Reviewers can tell. The talks that get accepted solve a specific problem the audience has, delivered by someone who's lived it.

Good first-talk topics:
- "How we solved [specific problem] at [scale]" — war stories from production
- "A practical guide to [tool/technique]" — hands-on, demo-able
- "What I learned failing at [thing]" — honest, contrarian

Bad first-talk topics:
- "The future of [broad field]" — too vague, no evidence
- "An introduction to [thing everyone knows]" — low value
- "Why [controversial opinion]" — hot takes without data get rejected

### Deadlines and Timing

CFPs typically run 4-8 months before the event. KubeCon CFPs close ~6 months out; smaller conferences closer to 3-4 months. Submit 2-3 weeks before the deadline — early enough to not rush, late enough to see what others are proposing (some CFP platforms show public submissions).

## Writing a Winning Proposal

### Title and Abstract

The title is what gets you read; the abstract is what gets you accepted. Both need to be specific and benefit-driven.

**Weak title:** "[Kubernetes Security](/articles/kubernetes-security-news-monthly)"
**Strong title:** "Locking Down K8s: A Defense-in-Depth Approach for Multi-Tenant Clusters"

**Weak abstract:** "This talk covers Kubernetes security best practices for production clusters."
**Strong abstract:** "Multi-tenant Kubernetes clusters face a unique threat surface: noisy neighbors, privilege escalation paths, and shared control planes. This talk walks through a defense-in-depth architecture tested across 40+ production clusters, covering network policy design, Pod Security Standards admission, and runtime detection with Falco. Attendees will leave with a concrete [hardening checklist](/articles/devsecops-hardening-checklist) and the trade-offs behind each control."

The second abstract tells reviewers exactly what the audience learns and signals you've done the work.

### Outline Structure

Most CFPs ask for an outline or talk structure. A reliable format:

1. **The problem (2-3 min)**: why this matters, who it affects
2. **Background (3-5 min)**: the minimum context needed to follow
3. **The approach (10-15 min)**: the meat — your solution, with specifics
4. **Demo or case study (5-8 min)**: proof it works
5. **Lessons and trade-offs (3-5 min)**: what didn't work, what you'd do differently
6. **Q&A (5-10 min)**: leave time for this

Include time estimates per section. Reviewers want to see you've thought about pacing.

### What Reviewers Look For

Talking to program committee members, the consistent themes:

- **Specificity over generality** — concrete examples beat abstract claims
- **A clear takeaway** — "attendees will learn X" should be explicit
- **Speaker fit** — evidence you have the experience to deliver this
- **Novelty** — not a rehash of last year's accepted talks (reviewers remember)
- **Practical applicability** — can the audience use this Monday morning?

<Callout type="info">
**Rejection is normal.** Acceptance rates at major conferences run 15-25%. Expect to be rejected multiple times before you're accepted. Each rejection with feedback makes the next proposal stronger. Submit to 3-5 CFPs in parallel.
</Callout>

## Slide Design for Technical Talks

### Code on Slides Done Right

The cardinal sin of technical talks: a full-screen screenshot of code no one can read. Rules for code on slides:

- **Maximum 8-10 lines per slide** — if it doesn't fit, it's too much
- **Font size 18pt minimum** — if the back row can't read it, it's decoration
- **Highlight the relevant line(s)** — dim everything except what you're discussing
- **Use a high-contrast theme** — dark background, light text reads well on projectors

```text
Good code slide structure:
1. Show the problem (2-3 lines of broken code)
2. Reveal the fix (highlight the changed line)
3. Explain why (one bullet, not a paragraph)
```

### Visual Storytelling

The best technical talks use visuals that a blog post couldn't:
- **Architecture diagrams** that build piece by piece (animate in)
- **Before/after comparisons** showing the impact
- **Timelines or flow diagrams** for processes
- **Real dashboards or logs** (redacted) showing the system in action

Avoid: stock photos of people pointing at whiteboards, walls of bullet points, screenshots of terminals with 200 lines.

### Avoiding Text Walls

A slide with 6+ bullet points is a document, not a presentation. Rules:
- **One idea per slide**
- **Max 3 bullets, max 6 words each** (the 6x6 rule, relaxed)
- **If you're reading your slides, the slides are doing your job** — cut the text

Slides should support what you say, not duplicate it. If the audience can get everything from the slides, they don't need you.

## Delivery and Stage Presence

### Rehearsal Strategy

- **Rehearse out loud, standing, 5+ times** — reading silently teaches you nothing about timing
- **Time every rehearsal** — most first-timers run 20% long; cut accordingly
- **Record yourself** — watch for filler words ("um," "so," "right") and pace
- **Rehearse with the demo** — live demos fail; rehearse the failure recovery
- **Do a dry run with colleagues** — their confusion reveals where you lost the thread

### Live Coding Tips

Live coding is high-risk, high-reward. If you must:
- **Pre-stage everything** — repos cloned, dependencies installed, terminals sized
- **Have a "known good" checkpoint** — a git tag you can reset to if things break
- **Type slowly and narrate** — the audience can't read as fast as you type
- **Consider a recording** — if the live demo is critical, pre-record it and narrate over the video; this is accepted practice, not cheating

Honestly: most live coding should be pre-recorded. The risk/reward rarely favors live.

### Handling Q&A

- **Repeat the question** — ensures the audience heard and buys you thinking time
- **It's okay to say "I don't know"** — followed by "but here's how I'd find out"
- **Redirect to the room** — "has anyone dealt with this?" surfaces audience expertise
- **Don't get defensive** — challenging questions aren't attacks; engage with the substance

## Post-Talk Leveraging

### Sharing Slides and Video

- **Post slides immediately** — on your site, Speaker Deck, or GitHub; link in the conference chat
- **Share the video when posted** — most conferences post talks within weeks; share on LinkedIn and your community
- **Write a companion blog post** — expands the talk into searchable content; drives traffic long after the event

### Building on the Talk

One talk should seed the next:
- **The Q&A reveals what people wanted more of** — that's your next talk
- **Repurpose for meetups** — a 45-minute conference talk becomes a 20-minute meetup talk
- **Turn it into a workshop** — if the talk generated interest, a paid workshop monetizes it

### Networking at the Event

The talk is the icebreaker; the hallway track is where the value compounds. After your talk:
- **Linger near the stage** — people with questions will find you
- **Go to the speaker dinner** — this is where collaborators and hiring managers are
- **Follow up within 48 hours** — connect on LinkedIn with a specific note ("great question about X")

<Callout type="tip">
**Movement break:** If you're rehearsing a talk, rehearse standing and walk during the parts where you're not demoing. Movement improves delivery and you'll be on your feet for 40 minutes on stage — train for it.
</Callout>

## Summary & Next Steps

Speaking compounds. One talk leads to invitations to others, to workshop offers, to the conversations that shape your career. Find a CFP this month — [cfp.land](https://cfp.land) is the place to start — and submit. The first acceptance is the hardest; every one after gets easier.

Wrist rests are one of those ergonomic products that people buy instinctively when their wrists hurt, assuming more padding must mean more comfort. The reality is more nuanced: a wrist rest can help, can be neutral, or can actively make things worse, depending on how you use it and which one you buy. The name itself is misleading — you're not supposed to rest your wrists on it while typing. This guide covers what wrist rests actually do, when they help, and how to choose one (or skip it) based on the ergonomics that genuinely matter.

## Do You Even Need One

The first thing to understand is the difference between **resting** and **planting**. A wrist rest is meant for the pauses between typing — a place to set your hands down so they're not hovering — not a surface to press into while you type. If you plant your wrists on the rest and type with your hands bent back (wrist extension), you've created exactly the static, strained posture that causes [wrist pain](/articles/ergonomic-mouse-wrist-pain-relief). The rest becomes the problem.

The goal is a **neutral wrist** — straight, not bent up, down, or sideways. When typing, your wrists should float just above the rest (or above the desk, if you have no rest), with your hands at roughly the same level as your forearms. The rest is there for the moments between, to reduce the static load of holding your hands up all day. If your keyboard is at the right height (elbows around 90 degrees, forearms roughly parallel to the floor) and you type with floating wrists, a rest is optional. If your keyboard is too high and you can't fix that, a rest can at least reduce the extension — but fixing the keyboard height is the better answer.

When a rest hurts: if you catch yourself pressing into it while typing, if it's too high (forcing extension) or too low (forcing flexion), or if it's so soft your wrists sink in and your hand angle becomes unstable. A rest that's the wrong height is worse than no rest, because it imposes a bad angle you wouldn't otherwise have. Measure your keyboard's front height before buying.

## Materials Compared

**Memory foam** is the most common and the most polarizing. It's soft and initially comfortable, but it can be too yielding — your wrists sink in, reducing support and creating an unstable angle. It also retains heat and can get clammy over a long session. Good for people who want softness and don't press into it; bad for anyone who plants their wrists, because the sinking creates extension.

**Wood** (or hard plastic) rests are firm and stable, providing consistent support without sinking. They're the choice if you want a predictable surface and don't need cushioning. The downside is no give, which some find uncomfortable for long resting periods, and they can feel cold initially. Many premium [mechanical keyboard](/articles/mechanical-keyboard-for-programmers) makers offer matching wood rests, which look great and perform well for the "rest between typing" use case.

**Gel** sits between foam and wood — firmer than foam, softer than wood, with a cool-to-the-touch feel. It's a reasonable middle ground for most users. The risk is cheaper gel rests that bottom out under pressure and behave like a hard surface with a thin cushion, offering the worst of both. Quality matters more with gel than with the other materials.

**Height and firmness** matter more than material. The rest should match your keyboard's front height so your wrists stay neutral when resting on it — measure before you buy, because "standard" heights vary. Firmness should be enough that your wrists don't sink more than a few millimeters; if they collapse into it, the angle changes and you've reintroduced the problem. **Breathability** is a minor but real factor for long sessions — fabric-covered rests breathe better than vinyl.

## Sizing and Placement

A wrist rest only works if it's the right size and in the right place. **Matching keyboard height** is the first rule — a rest that's taller or shorter than your keyboard's front edge forces an angle. Full-size and TKL keyboards have matching rests; for split or ergonomic keyboards, you often need a rest designed for that specific board, or two separate rests for each half.

**Width and alignment** — the rest should span the width of the keyboard (or the alphanumeric section for a mouse-side setup) and align so your wrists land on it naturally when your hands are on the home row. A rest that's too short leaves your outer fingers unsupported; one that's misaligned twists your wrist.

**Mouse-side considerations** — many people buy a keyboard rest and forget the mouse, but the mouse hand often hurts more, because mouse use involves more wrist extension and ulnar deviation. A mouse-side rest (or a mouse platform that supports the heel of your hand) matters at least as much as the keyboard rest. If you're buying one, buy both — supporting only the keyboard hand leaves the mouse hand worse off.

## Our Picks

Rather than specific brands (which change), here's how to choose. **Budget option**: a basic memory foam rest that matches your keyboard height — under $15, fine for casual use, acceptable if you don't plant your wrists. **Premium pick**: a firm wood or high-quality gel rest sized to your keyboard, from a maker that matches the height precisely — more expensive but stable and durable, the right call for a daily driver you'll use for years.

**When to skip entirely**: if your keyboard is at the correct height and you type with floating wrists, you may not need a rest at all — many ergonomic setups use no rest, relying on good keyboard height and a neutral posture instead. If your wrists don't hurt and your posture is neutral, save your money. The rest is a tool to achieve a neutral wrist, not a requirement — and if you already have that, adding a rest can only maintain or degrade it, not improve it. Buy one if you need the support between typing; skip it if you don't. The ergonomics are what matter; the rest is just one way to get there.

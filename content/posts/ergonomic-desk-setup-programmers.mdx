I learned about ergonomics the hard way — after a back injury that took me out of work for three weeks. Don't be like me. Set up your desk correctly before your body forces you to. This guide covers the key measurements and choices that make the biggest difference.

## The Cost of Bad Ergonomics

The statistics are sobering: 60-80% of software developers experience back pain, and RSI (repetitive [strain](/articles/eye-strain-reduction-screen-workers) injury) accounts for a significant portion of tech industry sick days. But the real cost isn't the acute pain — it's the chronic adaptation. Your body literally reshapes itself around your posture. Shortened hip flexors, rounded shoulders, and forward head position become your default, and fixing them later is much harder than preventing them now.

## Desk Height and Chair Setup

### Correct Desk Height

Your desk height should allow your elbows to rest at a 90-100 degree angle when typing. Here's the formula:

- **[Standing desk](/articles/monitor-arm-mount-standing-desk)**: Desk surface at elbow height (measure from floor to elbow crease)
- **Sitting desk**: Desk surface at elbow height when seated with feet flat

```text
Standing: Floor to elbow crease = desk height (typically 40-44 inches for most people)
Sitting: Seat pan to elbow crease = desk height (typically 26-28 inches)
```

### Chair Adjustments

1. **Seat height**: Feet flat on floor, knees at ~90 degrees
2. **Seat depth**: 2-3 fingers between the back of your knees and the seat edge
3. **Backrest**: Lumbar support at the curve of your lower back
4. **Armrests**: At elbow height when shoulders are relaxed — not raised

<Callout type="warning">
Armrests that are too high cause shoulder shrugging. Armrests that are too low cause slouching. Adjust them so your shoulders stay neutral.
</Callout>

## Monitor Placement

The "rule of thirds" for monitor placement:

- **Height**: Top of the screen at or slightly below eye level
- **Distance**: Arm's length away (20-30 inches from your eyes)
- **Angle**: Tilted back 10-20 degrees

### Dual Monitor Setup

If you use two monitors:
- **Equal use**: Place both monitors side by side, centered
- **Primary/secondary**: Place primary monitor directly in front, secondary at a 30-degree angle
- **Avoid**: Having one monitor off to the side requiring constant head turning — this causes neck strain

## Keyboard and Mouse Position

### Wrist Neutrality

Your wrists should be straight (neutral) when typing — not flexed up or down. This is where most keyboard trays and ergonomic keyboards earn their value.

- **Keyboard height**: At or slightly below elbow level
- **Keyboard angle**: Flat or slightly negatively tilted (front lower than back)
- **Mouse position**: Same level as keyboard, within easy reach

### Split Keyboard Considerations

Split keyboards (like the ErgoDox, Kinesis Advantage, or Microsoft Sculpt) reduce ulnar deviation — the sideways bending of your wrists. If you're experiencing pinky-side [wrist pain](/articles/ergonomic-mouse-wrist-pain-relief), a split keyboard is worth the adjustment period.

## Lighting and Environment

### Screen Glare Reduction

- Position your monitor perpendicular to windows (not directly facing or backing them)
- Use an anti-glare screen if overhead lighting causes reflections
- Adjust monitor brightness to match ambient light — not too bright in a dark room, not too dim in a bright one

### Ambient Lighting

- Avoid working in a dark room with only a bright monitor — this causes eye strain
- Use bias lighting (a light behind the monitor) to reduce contrast between screen and surroundings
- Target 300-500 lux ambient light for office work

### Break Reminders

Set up a visual or audio reminder to take breaks:
- **20-20-20 rule**: Every 20 minutes, look 20 feet away for 20 seconds
- **Micro-breaks**: Every 30-60 minutes, stand up or stretch for 30 seconds
- **Posture check**: Every hour, reset your posture — shoulders back, head back, feet flat

<Note>
The best ergonomic setup is the one you actually use. A $2000 standing desk won't help if you never adjust it. Start with the free fixes (monitor height, chair adjustment, keyboard position) before investing in equipment.
</Note>

Your desk setup is a tool, not a one-time project. As your work changes, your setup should adapt. The key is awareness — notice when something feels off, and fix it before it becomes pain.

Let's get one thing straight up front: "bad posture" isn't a moral failing, and the goal here isn't to sit ramrod-straight all day (nobody does that, and trying is exhausting). The real problem with hours at a desk is that you spend them in one shape — head forward, shoulders rounded, upper back stiff. Your body adapts to what you do most, and what you do most is stare at a screen. These [exercises](/articles/mobility-exercises-hip-flexors) are about giving your body the movements it's not getting, in small doses you'll actually do.

## Why Desk Posture Degrades

Two patterns dominate. **Forward head posture** — your head creeps toward the screen, and since your head weighs roughly 10–12 lbs, every inch forward multiplies the load on your neck muscles. **Rounded shoulders** — your shoulders roll forward and inward, tightening the chest and weakening the upper back. Both are the predictable result of hours in one position, not a weakness of character.

The fix isn't to hold a "correct" position through willpower — that lasts about 20 minutes. It's to add the missing movements frequently enough that your body doesn't lock into the desk shape. Think of it as movement nutrition: small, regular doses beat one heroic gym session.

## Quick Daily Reset Routine

Three movements, two minutes total. Do them once mid-morning and once mid-afternoon, or anchor them to a cue you already have (after a standup, when you refill water).

**Chin tucks** — sit tall, look straight ahead, and gently draw your chin straight back as if making a double chin. Hold 3 seconds, release. 10 reps. This wakes up the deep neck flexors that forward head posture turns off. It feels odd the first few times; that's the point.

**Wall angels** — stand with your back against a wall, heels a few inches out, bum and upper back touching. Arms in a "goalpost" shape against the wall, then slide them up and down, keeping contact. 10 slow reps. This opens the chest and gets the upper back moving in a direction it never goes at a desk.

**Thoracic extensions** — sit in a chair, clasp your hands behind your head, and gently arch your upper back over the chair back, opening the chest. Hold a few seconds, return. 8 reps. This counteracts the rounded-spine shape directly.

Anchor new movements to something you already do every day. "After I close my laptop for lunch" or "when the standup ends" beats a vague goal of "stretch more." Habits stick when they ride on existing routines — see our [habit-building guide](/blog/habit-building-irregular-schedules) for the mechanics.

## Building the Habit

The two-minute rule matters here. A routine you'll actually do for two minutes beats a 20-minute routine you skip. Start embarrassingly small — even just chin tucks alone, once a day — and let consistency build before you add more. The goal is a year of daily two-minute resets, not a perfect week followed by nothing.

Track it lightly if that helps, but don't turn it into pressure. A checkmark on a sticky note is plenty. The moment a wellness habit becomes a source of guilt, it stops happening — and we're aiming for the opposite of preachy here.

## When to Escalate

There's an important line between the discomfort of a stiff body and actual pain. These exercises help with the former. If you have sharp pain, numbness or tingling in your arms, or headaches that don't resolve, stop troubleshooting with YouTube and see a physiotherapist. Desk stiffness is normal; nerve symptoms aren't, and they're worth professional eyes.

And remember that exercises are the supplement, not the foundation. An [ergonomic desk setup](/blog/ergonomic-desk-setup-programmers) — screen at eye level, chair supporting your lower back, keyboard at the right height — does more for your posture than any exercise routine can overcome. Get the setup right, then use these movements to fill the gaps the setup can't address. That combination is what actually keeps a desk-bound body feeling decent over years, without anyone needing to become a wellness influencer about it.

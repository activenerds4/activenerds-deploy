A virtual hackathon lives or dies on its platform. In person, a lot of the event's function is handled by physical proximity — teams form by walking up to each other, mentors float between tables, the energy of the room carries people through the 3am slump. Online, every one of those functions has to be deliberately provided by the platform, and the platform that's great at one (say, slick submissions) is often weak at another (say, organic [team formation](/articles/hackathon-preparation-developers)). Choosing well is the difference between an event that produces real projects and one where half the participants disengage by hour six.

This guide compares the leading platforms, what each is actually good at, and how to choose based on your event's size, budget, and goals.

## What a Platform Needs

A virtual hackathon platform has to do four things, and most do some better than others.

**Team formation and chat.** The hardest part to replicate online. In person, teams self-organize; online, you need a structured way for solo participants to find teams and for teams to recruit. The platform should support a "looking for team" channel, team-formation matching (by skill, by idea), and persistent team chat that survives the event. Discord/Slack bolted on alongside a platform works but fragments the conversation; an integrated team chat keeps everything in one place, which matters when participants are juggling the platform, the chat, and their dev environment.

**Submission and judging.** The platform needs a structured submission flow (project name, description, demo video, code link, the categories entered) and a judging interface that lets judges see submissions, score against a rubric, and compare. This is the part most platforms do well because it's the most concrete — it's a forms-and-database problem. The variation is in how flexibly the rubric can be defined and how judges collaborate (individual scoring then aggregate, vs. discussion-based).

**Mentor coordination.** Mentors need a way to see which teams want help, claim requests, and route specialized questions. A help-queue model (teams request, mentors claim) is the standard; the question is whether the platform supports it natively or you bolt it onto a chat tool. Native is better — it keeps mentor activity visible to organizers, who can see which teams are stuck and intervene.

**The thing platforms miss: energy.** No platform provides the room energy that carries an in-person event. This is the gap you have to fill with the event design — kickoffs, checkpoints, a closing ceremony, live streams — because the platform won't. Treat the platform as the infrastructure; the energy is your job.

## Leading Platforms

The landscape has consolidated around a few platforms, each with a clear niche.

**Devpost** is the default for the broad hackathon ecosystem — it's where most university and community hackathons run, and participants often already have an account from a previous event, which lowers friction. Its strength is the submission-and-judging flow and the project gallery (a public showcase of past projects, which helps with post-event visibility and recruitment for future events). Its team formation is functional but not its strongest suit — many events use Discord alongside it for the social/team-forming side, which works but splits the experience. Best for: events that prioritize a smooth submission/judging flow and benefit from the existing Devpost participant base, especially university and community events.

**Major League Hacking (MLH)** runs the infrastructure for the large, recurring hackathon circuit — the weekend-long events with hundreds to thousands of participants. MLH provides the platform, the mentor network, the judging infrastructure, and the event-running expertise as a package. Its strength is scale and operational support; its limitation is that it's oriented toward the MLH-style event format (weekend, team-based, judged), which is great if that's your event and constraining if it isn't. Best for: large-scale weekend hackathons that want a turnkey operation and access to the MLH participant network.

**Dedicated enterprise tools.** A category of platforms (Devfolio, HackerEarth, and enterprise-focused tools) target corporate/internal hackathons and innovation challenges. Their strength is customization — branding, integration with corporate SSO, private events, custom judging workflows — and their limitation is cost and that they're overkill for a small community event. Best for: corporate hackathons, internal innovation events, and situations where you need the event to live inside your brand and identity system rather than on a public platform.

A note on the DIY approach: some teams build their event on Discord/Slack plus a Google Form for submissions and a spreadsheet for judging. This works for small events (under ~50 participants) and costs nothing, but it doesn't scale — the manual overhead of tracking submissions, routing mentors, and tabulating scores becomes the organizer's full-time job past a certain size. The platforms exist because they solve this; the DIY route is reasonable only for small events or as a one-off.

## Decision Factors

The choice comes down to a few factors, in roughly this order of importance.

**Scale and cost.** Devpost is free or low-cost for many event types; MLH is a partnership model; enterprise tools are priced for enterprise budgets. Match the platform's cost model to your event's budget and expected size. A free platform that doesn't scale to your participant count is a worse choice than a paid one that does — the failure mode is the platform buckling under load mid-event, which is unrecoverable.

**Customization vs simplicity.** How much do you need the event to look like *your* event, vs. is a standard hackathon format fine? If branding and custom workflows matter (corporate events, branded community events), lean toward the tools that support it. If a standard format is fine, the simplicity of Devpost or MLH is a feature — less to configure, fewer things to break.

**Integration with tooling.** Does the platform integrate with the tools your participants use (GitHub, Slack/Discord, your SSO)? Integration reduces friction at the moment it matters most — the start of the event, when participants are setting up and any friction costs you engagement. A platform that requires participants to create yet another account and learn a new interface loses people in the first hour; one that uses their existing GitHub identity and chat tool of choice keeps them.

## Running It Well

The platform is the infrastructure; the event design is what makes it work. A few practices that apply regardless of platform.

**Clear judging rubrics.** Publish the rubric at the start, not the end. Teams that know what they're being judged on build to it; teams that don't waste effort on dimensions that don't score. A good rubric is specific enough to be useful (not "creativity" but "novelty of approach to the problem domain") and weighted to reflect what you actually value. The rubric is also the judging tool — judges scoring against a shared rubric produce comparable scores, where judges scoring "overall impression" produce noise.

**Timezone fairness.** [Virtual events](/articles/virtual-tech-events-online-meetups) span timezones, and a "start at 9am" that's convenient for the organizer is 3am for some participants. Either design the event to be timezone-agnostic (asynchronous checkpoints over a longer window rather than a tight 24-hour sprint) or pick a start time that's tolerable across your participant geography and state it clearly upfront. The teams that drop out at hour four are often the ones for whom the event started at an unreasonable local hour.

**Post-event engagement.** The event ends, the projects get judged, and then... most hackathons lose the participants. The platform's project gallery is the start of post-event engagement — a public showcase that participants can share, which feeds recruitment for the next event. Follow up with participants (a survey, an invite to the next event, a showcase of winning projects) within a week, while the event is still fresh. The [community-building guide](/blog/organizing-tech-meetup-guide) covers the longer arc of turning event participants into an ongoing community.

The honest framing: there's no best platform, only the best fit for a specific event. Devpost for the smooth submission flow and existing participant base, MLH for large-scale turnkey events, enterprise tools for branded corporate events, and DIY for small one-offs. Choose based on your scale, budget, and how much customization you need — and remember that the platform provides the infrastructure, but the event's success is determined by the design: the rubric, the timezone handling, the energy you inject, and the follow-up. A great platform with a poorly designed event produces a forgettable event; a basic platform with a well-designed event produces one participants return to.

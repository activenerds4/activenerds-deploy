Blue light glasses are one of the most-marketed and least-understood products in the desk-worker wellness space. The claims range from "prevents eye strain" to "fixes your sleep" to "protects your retinas." The evidence is narrower than the marketing. This is an honest review of what blue light glasses actually do, what they don't, and when they're worth buying.

## The Blue Light Debate

### What Studies Show

The research on blue light and eye strain is surprisingly thin. Multiple systematic reviews — including a 2017 Cochrane-style review and subsequent studies — have found no strong evidence that blue light blocking lenses reduce digital eye strain. The symptoms people attribute to blue light (dry eyes, fatigue, headache) are more reliably explained by reduced blink rate, screen distance, and sustained focus.

### Sleep Impact Evidence

This is where the evidence is stronger. Blue light exposure in the evening suppresses melatonin production and shifts your circadian rhythm. Studies show that evening blue light exposure delays sleep onset and reduces sleep quality. Blocking that light — whether with glasses, screen filters, or lighting changes — can help, particularly for people who work late or use screens before bed.

### Eye Strain Claims

The honest summary: blue light glasses are unlikely to fix your daytime eye strain. If your eyes hurt after a long coding session, the culprit is almost certainly:
- Blinking 60% less than normal while focused on a screen
- Screen distance too close or too far
- Undiagnosed refractive error (you need glasses, just not blue-light ones)
- Dry air or screen glare

<Callout type="info">
If you have persistent eye strain, see an optometrist. A pair of computer glasses with the correct prescription and anti-reflective coating will do more than any blue light filter.
</Callout>

## What Glasses Actually Do

### Filtering Percentages

Blue light glasses filter a percentage of blue light, typically in the 380-500nm range. Filtering levels vary:
- **Clear lenses**: filter 10-20% of blue light (daytime use, minimal color distortion)
- **Yellow-tinted**: filter 50-90% (evening use, noticeable color shift)
- **Orange/red-tinted**: filter 95%+ (sleep-focused, strong color distortion)

Clear lenses marketed for daytime use filter so little blue light that their physiological effect is marginal. The heavier-tinted lenses that meaningfully shift melatonin are too color-distorted for coding.

### Lens Coating Types

- **Anti-reflective coating**: reduces glare from the screen and ambient light. This is the feature that [actually helps](/articles/posture-corrector-device-review) daytime comfort.
- **Blue-light filter coating**: the blue-blocking layer itself
- **Photochromic**: darkens in sunlight (irrelevant for indoor desk work)

The anti-reflective coating does more for screen comfort than the blue-light filter. If you buy glasses for daytime desk use, prioritize AR coating.

### Prescription Options

If you need vision correction, get blue light filtering built into prescription lenses. Non-prescription "computer glasses" with mild magnification (+0.25 to +0.75) are sometimes marketed for screen work — these help only if you have a specific accommodation issue, which an optometrist should diagnose.

## Product Reviews

### Gunnar Optiks

Gunnar is the most recognizable gaming-focused blue light brand. Their lenses have a slight yellow tint and proprietary coating.

**Key features:**
- Slight yellow tint (filters ~65% blue light)
- Anti-reflective coating
- Wrapped frame design (reduces air flow to eyes — helps with dryness, hurts ventilation)
- Prescription available

**Price:** $50-$150

**Best for:** Gamers and evening screen users who tolerate the slight tint. The wrapped frame helps dry eyes but some find it claustrophobic.

### Felix Gray

Felix Gray positions itself as a premium, style-forward option with clear lenses and a lighter filter.

**Key features:**
- Clear lenses (filters ~15-20% blue light)
- Anti-reflective coating
- Blue light filter embedded in lens (not a coating that can scratch off)
- Prescription available

**Price:** $95-$195

**Best for:** Daytime office use where you want clear lenses and a professional look. The low filter level means minimal sleep benefit.

### Budget Amazon Options

Generic Amazon brands ($15-$30) vary wildly in quality. Some have decent AR coatings; others are tinted plastic with no real filtering. Read reviews carefully and look for actual filtering percentages, not just "blue light blocking" marketing.

**Best for:** Trying the concept cheaply before committing to a premium pair.

## Alternatives That Work Better

### Night Shift / f.lux

Software that warms your screen's color temperature in the evening is free and more effective than clear-lens glasses for sleep. macOS Night Shift and Windows Night Light are built in; f.lux offers finer control.

```text
Recommended evening schedule:
- Sunset: begin warming to 3400K
- 2 hours before bed: 1900K (candle-like)
- Bedtime: 1200K (very warm, minimal blue)
```

This removes far more blue light than any clear-lens glasses, with zero cost and no color-distorting eyewear.

### Screen Positioning

Moving your monitor an arm's length away (20-26 inches) reduces eye strain more than blue light glasses. Closer screens force your eyes into sustained near-focus, which fatigues the ciliary muscles.

### Lighting Changes

The single most effective eye-strain intervention is balanced ambient lighting. A dark room with a bright monitor forces your pupils to constantly adjust. Add bias lighting (a soft light behind the monitor) to reduce the contrast between screen and room.

## Verdict

### Who Benefits Most

- **Evening screen users**: people who code or read on screens within 2-3 hours of bed benefit from blue light reduction for sleep — but software filters do this better than clear glasses
- **Glare-sensitive users**: the anti-reflective coating on any quality pair helps with screen glare
- **Dry-eye sufferers**: wrapped-frame glasses (like Gunnar) reduce air flow to the eyes, which helps dryness

### When to Buy vs Skip

**Buy if:**
- You work late and won't use software filters (some find f.lux too distracting)
- You want prescription computer glasses with AR coating anyway
- You have dry eyes and want a wrapped frame

**Skip if:**
- Your main complaint is daytime eye strain (address blink rate, distance, and prescription first)
- You expect them to "fix" fatigue (they won't)
- You'd use software filters instead

### Cost-Benefit Analysis

A $15 Amazon pair to test the concept is reasonable. A $150 premium pair for daytime use is hard to justify on the evidence — the same money spent on an optometrist visit and bias lighting does more. For evening use, free software beats paid glasses.

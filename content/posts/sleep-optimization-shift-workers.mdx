On-call rotations, late deploys, and follow-the-sun handoffs make a mockery of "just sleep 8 hours at the same time every night" advice. Shift work in tech is real, and the standard sleep hygiene tips assume a schedule you don't have. This guide is for the people whose week includes a 2am page, an early standup, and a weekend on-call — and who need sleep strategies that survive that reality.

## Why Shift Work Breaks Sleep

Your body runs on a circadian rhythm that expects roughly the same sleep window every 24 hours, cued by light and dark. Shift work forces sleep at biologically unusual times, and on-call work fragments it further — you don't just sleep at odd hours, you sleep *expecting* to be woken, which is its own kind of exhaustion.

The result is circadian misalignment: your body wants to be alert when you're trying to sleep, and wants to sleep when you're trying to work. Over weeks this accumulates into chronic sleep debt, which looks like irritability, slower thinking, and a shorter fuse — the exact opposite of what you need during an incident. This is also a core driver of the [on-call burnout](/blog/burnout-prevention-on-call) pattern, and fixing sleep is one of the highest-leverage things you can do for [sustainable pacing](/articles/sustainable-pacing-oncall-rotations).

## Anchor Sleep Strategy

The most effective technique for irregular schedules is **anchor sleep** — protecting a consistent core sleep window that you hit every single day, regardless of what else shifts around it. Even if your total sleep varies, keeping a fixed 4–5 hour block (say, 2am–7am, or whatever aligns with your most common work pattern) gives your circadian rhythm something stable to lock onto.

Naps are supplements, not replacements. A 20–30 minute nap before a night shift or after an on-call page takes the edge off without dumping you into deep sleep (which leaves you groggy). Keep naps short and earlier rather than later in your waking period. The 90-minute full-cycle nap is powerful but risky if you have to be functional immediately after.

Consistency on days off matters more than people expect. The temptation after a rough on-call week is to "catch up" by sleeping until noon on Saturday, but wildly different weekend sleep just re-fragments the rhythm you spent the week establishing. Try to keep your wake time within an hour or two of your weekday wake time, even on weekends.

## Light and Environment

Light is the strongest circadian cue you can control. **Morning light exposure** — getting bright light soon after waking — anchors your body's "day start." If you wake in the dark for an early shift, a bright light box for 15–20 minutes does the job the sun would. This is the single highest-leverage thing shift workers can do.

**Blocking blue light** before your sleep window helps your body accept that it's night, which matters when you're trying to sleep at 2pm. Dim screens an hour before, use warm lighting, and consider blue-light filtering — though as we covered in our [blue light glasses review](/blog/blue-light-glasses-effectiveness), the glasses are a minor tool; the bigger wins are reducing screen brightness and using warm light in the bedroom itself.

Your **bedroom setup** should be dark, cool, and quiet — and for shift workers, that often means investing in blackout curtains, a white noise machine, and earplugs, because you'll frequently sleep during daylight and during the world's noisiest hours. Treat these as work equipment, not luxuries; they directly affect your ability to function on shift.

## Recovery Routines

After an on-call page or a night shift, have a deliberate **wind-down** rather than trying to sleep immediately after a stressful incident. Your adrenaline is high; lying in bed willing yourself to sleep doesn't work. A 20–30 minute decompression — dim light, no screens, something repetitive — lets your nervous system settle. This is the same principle behind the recovery rituals in our [sustainable on-call pacing](/blog/sustainable-pacing-oncall-rotations) guide.

**Caffeine cutoff timing** matters more for shift workers. Caffeine has a half-life of 5–6 hours, so a coffee at the start of your last work hour can still be active when you try to sleep. Cut caffeine 6–8 hours before your intended sleep window, even if that means your "morning" coffee is at 6pm.

<Callout type="tip">
If you're consistently struggling to sleep, falling asleep at wrong times, or feeling exhausted despite adequate hours, talk to a doctor. Shift work sleep disorder is a real, diagnosable condition with treatments beyond willpower and blackout curtains. Don't white-knuckle a solvable problem.
</Callout>

Sleep under irregular schedules is never going to be as clean as the textbook version, and that's okay. The goal isn't perfect sleep hygiene — it's good-enough sleep, protected by a consistent anchor window, supported by light management, and recovered from deliberately after the inevitable disruptions. That's what keeps you functional over years of on-call, without pretending the schedule is something it isn't.

The standing desk has a predictable failure mode: someone gets one, stands for six hours the first day because standing is "healthier," and by day three their lower back and feet hurt so much they lower the desk and never raise it again. The desk didn't fail; the transition did. Standing is a load your body isn't adapted to if you've sat for years, and like any unaccustomed load, too much too fast produces the soreness that ends the experiment.

This is a ramp-up schedule that gets you to a sustainable sit-stand ratio without the week-three abandonment. It's not a fitness program; it's a dosing schedule for a new physical demand, applied with the same patience you'd apply to ramping a new workload into production.

## Why Ease Into It

What actually gets tired when you stand isn't what people expect. It's not your legs doing the work of standing — it's your calves, your feet, and the small stabilizing muscles of your hips and lower back, all of which are engaged isometrically to keep you upright. These muscles are undertrained in chronic sitters, and isometric holds fatigue them in a way that dynamic movement doesn't. The soreness you feel after a long stand is these muscles, not a sign that standing is bad for you.

The realistic timeline is two to three weeks to adapt to a meaningful standing portion, not two days. The people who succeed with standing desks are the ones who treat the first fortnight as adaptation, not as the target state. The ones who fail treat day one as the target state, get hurt, and conclude standing desks don't work. The desk is fine; the dose was wrong.

There's also a posture dimension that ramps separately. Standing well (weight distributed, knees soft, not locked) is a skill; standing badly (locked knees, hips thrust forward, weight on one leg) loads your joints in ways sitting doesn't, and it takes time to build the habit of standing well before you stand for long. Easing in lets you build the posture habit at low volume, when you can pay attention to it, rather than trying to fix it at high volume when you're tired and defaulting to bad positions.

## Week-by-Week Ramp

**Week 1 — baseline.** Don't stand more than 30-45 minutes at a time, and no more than two hours total standing in a day. The goal this week is to discover what gets sore and to practice standing well, not to accumulate standing time. Stand for one focus block, sit for two, repeat. If anything hurts (not just tired — actually hurts), you stood too long; cut back. By the end of the week, your calves and feet should be mildly tired but not sore, and you should have a feel for what good standing posture is.

**Week 2 — adding blocks.** Increase to 60 minutes per standing block, up to three hours total. You're building the capacity to stand through a full focus block without it being the dominant experience of the block. Start alternating more aggressively: stand for a block, sit for a block. The transition itself — the act of changing position — is part of the benefit, so frequent changes matter more than total standing time. By the end of week 2, standing for an hour should feel unremarkable.

**Week 3 — listening to fatigue.** Push to 4-5 hours total if you feel good, but the rule this week is to stop a standing block the moment you notice you're shifting weight constantly or leaning. That's fatigue, and pushing through it is how you build bad posture habits. The signal to sit isn't pain; it's the fidgeting that precedes pain. If you respond to fidgeting by sitting, you never reach pain, and the next standing block starts fresh.

The common mistake at every stage is pushing through fatigue because you're "supposed to" stand more. You're not. The goal is a sustainable ratio you can maintain for years, not a number you hit today and abandon tomorrow. Under-shooting the standing time and feeling good beats over-shooting and hurting, every time.

## Posture and Footwear

Weight distribution is the single biggest factor in whether standing helps or hurts. Weight should be evenly distributed between both feet, roughly mid-foot to heel, with knees soft (not locked — locked knees transfer the load to your lower back and reduce circulation). Hips should be stacked over the ankles, not thrust forward (anterior pelvic tilt) or tucked under (posterior tilt). Shoulders over hips. If you find yourself leaning on the desk or shifting to one leg, you're fatigued — sit.

The anti-fatigue mat is the one accessory that genuinely helps. A mat with some give lets your feet make micro-adjustments, which keeps the stabilizing muscles lightly active rather than locked, and it reduces the foot and calf soreness that ends standing sessions early. It's not magic, but it's the difference between standing on concrete and standing on something forgiving, and for the cost it's the highest-leverage [standing desk accessory](/blog/standing-desk-mat-comparison). You don't need an expensive one; you need one with actual cushion.

Shoes versus barefoot: barefoot or socks-only standing sounds natural but is harder on most floors (which are hard), and it offers no cushion. A shoe with a modest, flexible sole gives you the cushion and the slight instability that keeps the feet working. Avoid stiff shoes (boots, thick soles) — they defeat the micro-adjustment that makes standing active rather than static. If you stand a lot, the shoe matters more than people expect.

## Making It Sustainable

The sit-stand ratio target is a guideline, not a rule, and the research is weaker than the marketing suggests. The honest summary: any ratio that gets you changing position regularly is fine; the goal is to break up long static periods, not to hit a specific standing quota. A common sustainable target is roughly 2:1 sit-to-stand by time, with a position change every 30-60 minutes. But if 1:1 works for you, do that; if 3:1 works, do that. The ratio that works is the one you'll actually maintain.

Pairing with movement breaks is what makes standing desks actually healthy rather than just less bad. Standing still for an hour is better than sitting still for an hour, but it's still being still. The real benefit comes when the standing block ends with a [movement break](/blog/micro-break-routines-coding-focus) — a walk, some [desk stretches](/blog/desk-stretches-coding-sessions), anything that uses the muscles dynamically. The [standing desk](/articles/standing-desk-benefits-drawbacks) is the setup; the movement is the payoff.

When standing isn't the answer: if you have lower-back issues that standing aggravates, if you have foot or ankle problems that standing worsens, or if your work requires fine motor control that's harder standing (some people find precise mouse work harder standing), then less standing is correct. [Standing desks](/articles/best-standing-desks-developers) are a tool for breaking up sitting, not a moral obligation, and the [benefits and drawbacks](/blog/standing-desk-benefits-drawbacks) are real on both sides. The schedule above is for people who want to stand more and keep hurting themselves by ramping too fast; if you've tried a sane ramp and standing still doesn't suit you, that's a fine conclusion, not a failure.

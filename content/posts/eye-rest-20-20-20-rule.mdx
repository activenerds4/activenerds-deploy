If you've spent a decade staring at screens, you know the feeling: by late afternoon your eyes are dry, gritty, and vaguely tired in a way that isn't sleepiness. It's not your eyes degrading in any permanent way, mostly — it's the predictable result of asking an organ designed to shift focus constantly to lock onto a fixed distance for hours. The 20-20-20 rule is the simplest, cheapest intervention for this, and it works for a concrete mechanical reason that has nothing to do with the wellness marketing around it.

This is the non-preachy version: what's actually happening to your eyes, why the rule helps, how to actually remember to do it (the hard part), and what else moves the needle beyond the rule.

## Why Screens Strain Eyes

Three things happen when you stare at a screen, and the 20-20-20 rule addresses two of them. The first is **reduced blink rate**. You blink about 15 times a minute normally; in front of a screen it drops to 5-7. Each blink spreads a fresh tear film across the cornea; with a third as many blinks, the film evaporates faster than it's replenished, and you get the dry, gritty feeling. This is the dryness component, and the rule helps it indirectly — looking away tends to coincide with a natural blink.

The second is **focusing fatigue**. The ciliary muscle inside your eye adjusts the lens to focus at different distances. Focusing close (screen distance, ~50cm) requires the muscle to contract; focusing far requires it to relax.

Holding a close focus for hours is a sustained contraction, and like any sustained muscle contraction, it fatigues. The ache and the difficulty refocusing when you look up from a long session are this muscle tired of being contracted. The rule addresses this directly — looking far relaxes the muscle.

The third is **dryness from airflow and glare**, which the rule doesn't address. Air conditioning and fans blow across the eye and accelerate tear evaporation; screen glare makes you squint, which reduces blink rate further. These are environmental, and they're fixed by environment (airflow direction, screen position, lighting), not by a break rule. The 20-20-20 rule is a focusing and blink intervention, not a complete eye-care protocol.

## How the Rule Works

The rule: every 20 minutes, look at something 20 feet (about 6 meters) away for 20 seconds. The numbers are mnemonic, not precise — the principle is "regularly look far for a bit," and the exact distances and durations are approximations that work.

The mechanics: looking 20 feet away is far enough that the ciliary muscle fully relaxes (the eye is essentially at rest focus past about 20 feet). Twenty seconds is long enough for the muscle to actually relax and for the tear film to redistribute with the blinks you'll naturally take. Twenty minutes is short enough that you intervene before the fatigue compounds, and long enough that it's not constant interruption. The "20 feet" part is the active ingredient; the "20 seconds" is enough time; the "20 minutes" is the dosing interval.

What counts as 20 feet: a window and whatever's outside it, the far wall of a large room, the other end of an open office. It doesn't need to be exactly 20 feet — further is fine, slightly less is fine, the point is "far enough that your focus relaxes." If you're in a tiny room with no window, the far wall of the room (even if only 10 feet) is better than the screen, and a deliberate look out a door or down a hallway works. The constraint is usually environmental, not effort.

Why distance matters specifically: it's not "looking away from the screen" that helps — it's "focusing far." Glancing at your phone is looking away from the screen, but it's another close focus, so it doesn't relax the ciliary muscle at all. The break has to be to a far distance, which is why "look out a window" is the practical version and "look at your phone" is useless for this purpose.

## Actually Remembering It

The barrier isn't knowing the rule; it's that 20 minutes passes without you noticing, and by the time you remember, it's been two hours. The fix is to externalize the cue so you don't have to remember.

**Timer-based.** The simplest version: a recurring 20-minute timer. The downside is that it interrupts deep work at arbitrary points, which is annoying enough that people disable it. A better version is a timer that only fires when you've been active (typing/mousing) for 20 minutes, so it doesn't fire during a meeting or a break. Some focus apps do this; a basic OS timer is the low-tech version.

** Better than a timer: attach the break to something that already happens. After each commit. After each standup.

At the top of each hour. The break rides on a cue you already respond to, which is how [habit-building](/blog/habit-building-irregular-schedules) actually works — you don't build a new trigger, you attach to an existing one. The downside is the interval isn't exactly 20 minutes, but the rule is a guideline, and "every hour, on the hour, look out the window for 20 seconds" is close enough and far more likely to stick.

**[Habit stacking](/articles/micro-exercise-routines-developers) with other breaks.** The most robust version: stack the 20-20-20 with the [micro-breaks](/blog/micro-break-routines-coding-focus) you should be taking anyway. Every time you take a movement break, you also look far. The breaks reinforce each other, and you get one cue that triggers two good things. This is the version that survives long-term, because it doesn't ask you to add a new behavior, just to add a component to a behavior you're already trying to do.

## Beyond the Rule

The 20-20-20 rule addresses focusing fatigue and partly addresses dryness, but the environmental factors that cause [digital eye strain](/blog/eye-strain-reduction-screen-workers) need environmental fixes. **Lighting**: the screen shouldn't be the brightest or dimmest thing in your field of view — aim for ambient light roughly comparable to the screen, and avoid a bright window directly behind or in front of the monitor. **Glare**: position the screen so light sources don't reflect off it; an anti-glare monitor or a matte screen coating helps. **Display settings**: brightness matched to the room (not maxed), a comfortable text size (if you're squinting, the text is too small), and a color temperature that's not blindingly blue in a dark room.

The blue-light question comes up here, and the honest answer is that blue-light blocking glasses have weak evidence for eye strain (the strain is from focusing and dryness, not [blue light](/articles/blue-light-glasses-effectiveness) per se), though they may help with sleep if worn in the evening. The [blue-light glasses](/blog/blue-light-glasses-effectiveness) question is separate from the eye-strain question, and conflating them leads to buying glasses that don't fix the problem you bought them for. Fix the focusing and the environment first.

When to see an optometrist: if you're doing the rule and the environmental fixes and still getting headaches, blurred vision that doesn't resolve, or eye pain, that's not eye strain — that's a reason to get your eyes checked. Uncorrected refractive error (needing glasses, or needing an updated prescription) produces symptoms that look exactly like eye strain and that no break rule will fix. The 20-20-20 rule is for healthy eyes under screen load; it doesn't substitute for correcting an actual vision problem, and many "chronic eye strain" cases turn out to be a prescription that's overdue for an update.

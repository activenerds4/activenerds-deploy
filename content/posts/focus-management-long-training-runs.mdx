<Callout variant="tip" title="Key Takeaways">
- **Stop watching, start alerting**: configure alerts and trust them; dashboard-refreshing is wasted attention
- **Schedule checkpoint reviews**: bounded, regular reviews beat continuous watching
- **Use wait time for shallow work**: code review, triage, docs; batch it into wait windows
- **Move on the checkpoint cadence**: stand for reviews, walk after milestones
- **Don't panic on failure**: diagnose, fix, resume from checkpoint; most runs are recoverable
- **Decompress post-run**: the walk after prevents cumulative fatigue
</Callout>

A long ML training run is a strange cognitive challenge. You're not actively coding for hours, but you can't fully disengage either — the model might diverge, OOM, or silently plateau while you're not watching. So you hover: refreshing loss curves, checking GPU utilization, half-working on something else with one eye on the dashboard. This split-attention state is the worst of both worlds — you're not productive and you're not rested. This guide is about breaking that pattern: how to monitor without obsessing, use the wait time well, and keep your brain and body functional through an 8-hour training run.

## The Long Run Challenge

### Cognitive Load of Monitoring

Watching training metrics is cognitively expensive in a sneaky way. Each refresh is a micro-decision: is the loss going down? Is that spike normal? Should I stop? You're not doing hard work, but you're making dozens of small judgments per hour — the kind of fragmented attention that drains you without producing anything.

### Interruption Patterns

Training runs interrupt in two ways:
- **Expected checkpoints**: every N epochs, you review and decide whether to continue
- **Unexpected failures**: OOM, NaN loss, divergence, hardware errors

The expected ones you can plan around. The unexpected ones are what keep you tethered — the fear that you'll miss a failure and waste hours of GPU time.

### Opportunity Cost of Watching

The core insight: watching a training run doesn't make it train faster. Every minute you spend refreshing the dashboard is a minute you're not doing something valuable. The goal isn't to watch more — it's to watch less, but at the right moments.

<Callout type="info">
**The reframing:** Treat a training run like a slow background job, not a live process. You don't `tail -f` a build for 20 minutes; you set it running and check back. Apply the same discipline to training.
</Callout>

## Monitoring Without Obsessing

### Alert-Based Monitoring

The fix for dashboard-refreshing: alerts, not observation. Configure your monitoring to tell you when something matters, rather than you checking constantly.

**Alerts to set up before a long run:**
- **Loss is NaN or inf** — immediate alert, kill the run
- **Loss hasn't improved in N epochs** — plateau detection
- **GPU utilization drops below 80%** — likely a data loading bottleneck or hang
- **Validation loss diverges from training loss** — overfitting
- **OOM or CUDA error** — immediate alert

With these alerts, you can stop watching and trust the system to ping you when needed. Use whatever your stack supports — W&B alerts, Prometheus alertmanager, a simple Slack webhook from your training script.

### Checkpoint Review Cadence

Instead of continuous watching, schedule checkpoint reviews:
- **Every checkpoint** (e.g., every epoch or N steps): a 2-minute review — loss trend, val metrics, any anomalies
- **Every 30-60 minutes**: a 5-minute deeper review — learning rate schedule, gradient norms, samples
- **At major milestones** (e.g., halfway, before LR decay): a 15-minute evaluation

This converts open-ended watching into bounded, scheduled reviews. Between them, you're free.

### Dashboard Design

A good monitoring dashboard reduces decision load. A bad one creates it. Design principles:
- **One screen, no scrolling** — everything visible at once
- **Loss curves with log scale** — early training dynamics are invisible on linear scale
- **Train and val on the same plot** — divergence is the key signal
- **Resource utilization** (GPU, memory, disk) — catches bottlenecks
- **Time-to-completion estimate** — removes the "how much longer" anxiety
- **No more than 6 panels** — more panels = more decisions

## Using Wait Time Productively

### Shallow Work Batching

The wait time between checkpoints is perfect for shallow work that doesn't require deep focus:
- **Code review** — PRs you've been putting off
- **Email and Slack triage** — batch it during waits, not during deep work
- **Documentation** — write the docstring you skipped
- **Issue triage** — label and prioritize GitHub issues

The key: batch these into the wait windows, don't let them bleed into your deep work blocks. A training run's wait time is a natural Pomodoro — use the rhythm.

### Reading and Research

Long runs are prime reading time — but read things that tolerate interruption:
- **Papers in your area** — but skim, don't deep-read (you'll be interrupted)
- **Tool documentation** — the library you've been meaning to learn
- **Engineering blogs** — from companies doing similar work
- **Not**: a dense theoretical paper requiring sustained focus

Save the deep reading for when you're not on-call to a training run.

### Code Review Windows

If you're part of a team, training runs are a great time to be the responsive reviewer:
- **Pick up PRs during wait windows** — you're at your desk anyway
- **Your faster reviews unblock teammates** — goodwill that compounds
- **Reviewing others' code is a break from your own** — different cognitive mode

This turns a passive wait into team value.

## Movement During Training

### Inter-Epoch Stretches

The sedentary risk of a training run is real — you're at your desk for hours, but without the natural micro-breaks that coding provides (compile times, test runs). Build them in deliberately:

<Callout type="tip">
**Inter-epoch movement protocol:**
- **Every checkpoint review**: stand for the 2-minute review (laptop on a high counter, or just stand and check on a second screen)
- **Every 60 minutes**: a 5-minute [movement snack](/articles/movement-snack-checklist-desk-workers) — see our [movement snack checklist](/blog/movement-snack-checklist-desk-workers)
- **Every 2-3 hours**: a 10-minute walk — outside if possible
- **Post-run**: a real decompression walk, not back to your desk
</Callout>

The checkpoint cadence gives you a natural trigger. Pair the review with movement — stand for the review, walk after a major milestone. See our [micro-break routines](/blog/micro-break-routines-coding-focus) for the full system.

### Walking Thinking Time

Some of the best thinking during a training run happens away from the screen. When you're stuck on a design decision or a bug, a 10-minute walk often resolves it. The training run gives you permission to step away — use it. Walking:
- **Restores attention** — breaks the screen-staring loop
- **Enables diffuse thinking** — the mode where connections happen
- **Counts as movement** — you're hitting two goals at once

### Post-Run Decompression

When a long run finishes (successfully or not), don't immediately dive into the next thing:
- **A 15-30 minute walk** — metabolize the stress of the run
- **Hydrate and eat** — you've probably forgotten
- **Review what happened** — while it's fresh, note the lessons
- **Then** start the next task

The post-run decompression is what prevents the cumulative fatigue that makes the next run harder.

## Failure Recovery Mindset

### OOM Without Panic

OOM during a long run is gutting — hours of GPU time, gone. But panic wastes the [recovery time](/articles/managing-context-switching-engineering). Protocol:
1. **Don't immediately restart** — you'll repeat the mistake
2.

**Save what you can** — checkpoints before the OOM are still valid
3. **Diagnose** — was it batch size, memory leak, gradient accumulation? See our [debugging OOM guide](/blog/debugging-oom-pytorch)
4.

Most OOMs are recoverable from a checkpoint. The hours aren't fully lost.

### Debugging Under Time Pressure

When a run fails and you're on a deadline (shared GPUs, paper submission, demo), the pressure distorts your debugging. Counter it:
- **Slow down to speed up** — 10 minutes of careful diagnosis beats 2 hours of frantic changes
- **Change one thing at a time** — panic debugging changes 5 things and you don't know what worked
- **Keep a log of what you tried** — under stress you'll forget
- **Ask for help** — a fresh pair of eyes is faster than your stressed ones

### Starting Over Efficiently

Sometimes you have to restart. Make it efficient:
- **Resume from checkpoint** if possible — don't retrain from scratch
- **Reduce the scope** — if you're out of time, train on a subset or for fewer epochs
- **Fix the config first** — restarting with the same broken config wastes another run
- **Document the failure** — so the next run doesn't repeat it

<Callout type="info">
**The meta-lesson:** Training runs teach you about your own work patterns as much as about your model. The engineers who handle long runs well are the ones who've learned to manage their own attention, not just their GPUs.
</Callout>

## Summary & Next Steps

A long training run is a test of your ability to manage attention as much as your model's ability to learn. The engineers who handle them well aren't the ones who watch closest — they're the ones who set up good alerts, use the wait time deliberately, move their bodies, and recover gracefully from failure.

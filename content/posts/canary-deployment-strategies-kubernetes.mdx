A deployment strategy is a bet about how much risk you can absorb. A rolling update bets that the new version is fine and accepts that if it's not, some users hit the broken version before you notice. A canary deployment bets the opposite: that you don't know if the new version is fine, so you send it a small fraction of traffic, watch what happens, and only proceed if nothing breaks. The canary is the strategy for when the cost of a bad deploy is higher than the cost of deploying slowly — which, for most production services, is most of the time.

This is the practical guide to canary deployments on Kubernetes — why they beat rolling updates for risky changes, how the traffic splitting works, how to gate on metrics, and the tooling choices.

## Why Canary Over Rolling

The rolling update is the Kubernetes default: it gradually replaces old pods with new ones, and if the new pods fail health checks, the rollout pauses. This is fine for changes you're confident in — bug fixes, small features — and dangerous for changes you're not. The failure mode of a rolling update is that "the new pods pass health checks" doesn't mean "the new version is serving users correctly." A version can pass health checks (the endpoint responds) while being broken in a way that only real traffic reveals: a latency regression, an error rate spike on a specific request type, a dependency that's only called in production. By the time these show up, the rolling update has put the new version in front of a significant fraction of users.

The blast radius question is what canary answers. A canary sends the new version to a small fraction of users (5%, say), watches the metrics that matter for a few minutes, and only proceeds if they're healthy. If the new version has a latency regression, it affects 5% of users for a few minutes, you catch it, and you roll back — instead of 50% of users for an hour. The blast radius is bounded by the canary fraction and the analysis window, both of which you control.

When canary is overkill: for changes you're highly confident in and where the cost of slow deployment is real. A typo fix in a non-critical service doesn't need a canary; the rolling update is fine, and the canary adds time and complexity for no benefit. Canary earns its complexity for changes with real risk: new dependencies, schema changes, significant logic changes, anything that's burned you before. Match the strategy to the risk, not to a policy that everything must canary.

## Traffic Splitting Mechanics

The core of a canary is weighted routing: a fraction of requests go to the new version, the rest to the stable version, and you control the fraction. There are two ways to implement this in Kubernetes, and the choice has real tradeoffs.

**Service mesh (Istio, Linkerd).** The service mesh sits between services and can split traffic by weight at the proxy level. You declare that 95% of traffic goes to the stable version and 5% to the canary, and the mesh's proxies enforce it. This is the most flexible approach — you can split by weight, by header (route specific users to the canary), by any rule the mesh supports — but it requires a service mesh, which is its own operational burden. If you already run a mesh, this is the natural way to canary; if you don't, installing one just for canaries is overkill.

**Ingress controller (NGINX, ALB, etc.).** Many ingress controllers support weighted traffic splitting across backends, which gives you canary without a full mesh. You declare multiple backends with weights, and the ingress splits traffic accordingly. This is simpler than a mesh but less flexible — header-based routing and complex rules are harder, and the feature varies by ingress controller. For many teams, ingress-level canary is the right balance: enough capability for weighted rollouts without the mesh overhead.

**Header-based routing** is the precision version: instead of splitting by percentage, you route specific requests (identified by header, cookie, user ID) to the canary. This lets you canary to internal users first, or to a specific test cohort, before exposing any real users. It's more controlled than percentage-based splitting and is the right approach for high-risk changes where you want to test with a known group before a random fraction of production. Most meshes and some ingress controllers support it.

## Metrics and Auto-promotion

The canary is only as good as the metrics you gate on. Sending traffic to the canary and hoping for the best is a rolling update with extra steps; the value is in measuring whether the canary is healthy and automatically proceeding or rolling back based on those measurements.

**Analysis windows.** Define how long the canary runs at each fraction before promotion — e.g., 5% for 5 minutes, then 25% for 5 minutes, then 100%. The window needs to be long enough to collect meaningful data (enough requests to distinguish a real regression from noise) and short enough to catch problems fast. For high-traffic services, a few minutes gives thousands of requests and clear signal; for low-traffic services, you may need longer windows or a larger canary fraction to get enough data. The window is a function of your traffic volume.

**Prometheus queries.** The metrics that gate promotion are usually error rate and latency, queried from Prometheus. "Canary error rate < stable error rate + threshold" and "canary p99 latency < stable p99 latency + threshold" are the typical checks. If the canary fails either, it doesn't promote; if it passes, it proceeds to the next step. The thresholds matter — too tight and normal variance fails the canary; too loose and real regressions pass. Tuning them to your service's normal variance is part of the setup, and it's worth doing on a service with known behavior before relying on it for a risky change.

**Auto-rollback triggers.** The point of the automation is that a failed canary rolls back without a human. Define the conditions (error rate above X for Y minutes) that trigger an automatic rollback to the stable version, and let the tooling execute it. The alternative — a human watching a dashboard and deciding — reintroduces the latency and error that automation is meant to remove. Auto-rollback is where the canary pays for itself: a bad deploy is detected and reverted in minutes, by the system, at 3am, without anyone being paged until after it's fixed.

## Tooling Choices

**[Argo Rollouts](/articles/progressive-delivery-argo-rollouts).** The CNCF project that's become the default for many teams. It's a Kubernetes controller that introduces a `Rollout` CRD (a richer Deployment) that supports canary (and [blue-green](/articles/blue-green-deployment-kubernetes)) strategies with traffic splitting and metric analysis. It integrates with Prometheus for metric gating and with service meshes and ingress controllers for traffic splitting. The appeal is that it's Kubernetes-native (your canary is a resource you apply, like everything else) and it works with the tools you likely already have. If you're starting canary work today, this is the default recommendation.

**Flagger.** The alternative, with a different philosophy. Flagger is a controller that automates the canary process on top of existing Deployments and service mesh/ingress — you don't change your workload resources, you add a Flagger `Canary` resource that orchestrates the rollout. It's tightly integrated with service meshes (it was built in the Flux/Weave ecosystem) and is a strong choice if you're already on Flux for GitOps. The choice between Argo Rollouts and Flagger often comes down to which ecosystem you're in (Argo vs Flux) — both are capable, and the decision is more about consistency with your existing tooling than about feature gaps.

**Build vs buy.** It's possible to build a canary with a shell script, a weighted service, and a Prometheus query, and for a simple service it might even work. The reason to use Argo Rollouts or Flagger is that canary logic has a long tail of edge cases (what if the metrics source is down, what if the canary and stable have different traffic patterns, what if a rollback happens mid-promotion) that the tools have already solved. Building it yourself means solving those edge cases yourself, which is rarely a good use of time. Use the tool; the canary pattern is well-trodden and the tools are mature.

The honest framing: canary deployments are the deployment strategy for changes with real risk, and the tooling to do them well on Kubernetes is mature. The investment is in setting up the traffic splitting, defining the metrics, and tuning the thresholds — once that's done, every risky deploy benefits. Pair this with [GitOps](/blog/gitops-argocd-flux-comparison) for the delivery mechanism, [CI/CD optimization](/blog/cicd-pipeline-optimization) for the build, and [SLOs](/blog/slo-definition-reliability) for the metrics you gate on, and the deployment pipeline becomes something you can trust with risky changes at 3am — which is the actual goal.

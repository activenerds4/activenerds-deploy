A large model that's accurate but slow is a deployment problem dressed up as a model problem. You can throw GPUs at it, or you can make the model smaller. Quantization shrinks weights; pruning removes them; distillation teaches a small model to behave like a big one. Of the three, distillation is the most underused and often the most effective — it transfers the *behavior* of a capable teacher into a student that's cheap enough to actually ship.

This guide covers why distillation works, the teacher-student framework, and the practical decisions that determine whether you get a fast model that's nearly as good or a fast model that's meaningfully worse.

## Why Compress Models

Inference cost is the constraint that actually bites. A 70B-parameter model might hit your accuracy target, but if it costs $0.05 per query and adds 800ms of latency, it's not viable for a high-traffic endpoint. The gap between "works in a notebook" and "works in production" is almost always about inference, and compression is how you close it.

The three compression approaches aren't interchangeable. Quantization reduces the precision of weights and activations — it's the cheapest to apply and gives a solid speed/size win, but it doesn't change the architecture, so you still pay for the full parameter count. Pruning removes parameters entirely but can leave you with irregular sparsity that's hard to accelerate on standard hardware. Distillation trains a *different, smaller* model to mimic the teacher, so you get a genuinely smaller architecture that runs fast on commodity hardware.

Distillation fits when you have a strong teacher (often a large model or an ensemble) and you need a student that fits a latency or memory budget the teacher can't meet. It's complementary to the others — a common production pattern is distill first (get a small architecture), then quantize the student (shrink it further). The [quantization guide](/blog/model-quantization-techniques) covers that second step; here we focus on the first.

## The Teacher-Student Framework

The core idea: instead of training the student on hard labels (the ground-truth class), train it to match the teacher's *soft* output distribution. A teacher that's 90% confident in class A and 8% in class B is telling you something the hard label "A" doesn't — that B is a close second. That extra signal is what Hinton called "dark knowledge," and it's why a student trained on soft labels can outperform one trained from scratch on hard labels alone.

Temperature scaling is the mechanism that exposes this signal. Softmax with temperature `T` softens the distribution: higher `T` flattens it, revealing more of the relative structure between classes. You train both teacher and student with the same elevated temperature, so the student learns the softened distribution, then use `T=1` at inference.

The loss is typically a weighted combination of two terms: a distillation loss (KL divergence between the teacher's and student's softened distributions) and a standard task loss (cross-entropy against the hard labels). The task loss keeps the student grounded in the actual objective; the distillation loss transfers the teacher's richer structure. The weight between them is a key hyperparameter — too much distillation and the student over-imitates the teacher's quirks; too little and you lose the transfer benefit.

```python
import torch.nn.functional as F

def distillation_loss(student_logits, teacher_logits, labels, T, alpha):
    soft = F.kl_div(
        F.log_softmax(student_logits / T, dim=1),
        F.softmax(teacher_logits / T, dim=1),
        reduction="batchmean",
    ) * (T * T)
    hard = F.cross_entropy(student_logits, labels)
    return alpha * soft + (1 - alpha) * hard
```

The `T * T` scaling on the soft loss matters: gradients of the softened softmax shrink by `1/T`, so you multiply back to keep the loss on the same scale as the hard term.

## Training a Student Model

The student's architecture is the single biggest decision. Too small and it lacks capacity to absorb the teacher's knowledge; too large and you've defeated the purpose. A common heuristic is a student with 10–20% of the teacher's parameters, but the right ratio depends on the task — a simpler task tolerates a smaller student. For language models, distilling to a smaller layer count or hidden dimension is typical; for vision, a shallower or narrower backbone.

The data you distill on matters more than people expect. Distillation transfers the teacher's behavior on the *training distribution*, so if the teacher is good on a narrow slice and you distill only on that slice, the student inherits the narrowness. Use the broadest representative dataset you can. Augmentation helps the student generalize, but apply it consistently to both teacher and student inputs so the teacher's soft labels match what the student sees.

Balancing hard and soft targets is where most distillation runs go sideways. Pure distillation (only the soft loss) can produce a student that mimics the teacher's confidences without actually solving the task well on hard predictions. Pure hard-label training throws away the transfer. Start with `alpha` around 0.7 (weighting distillation heavily) and tune down if the student's hard-label accuracy lags. Watch both the distillation loss and the task accuracy — if the former drops nicely but the latter plateaus below the teacher, the student is learning to imitate without learning to perform.

## Measuring the Gains

Distillation only matters if it produces a deployable win. Measure three things: latency, size, and accuracy retention. Latency is the point — benchmark the student on the actual target hardware (GPU, CPU, edge), not just in a notebook, because the speedup depends heavily on the deployment. Size is straightforward (parameter count, disk footprint). Accuracy retention is the ratio of student accuracy to teacher accuracy on your held-out set.

A good distillation run retains 95–99% of the teacher's accuracy at a fraction of the latency. If you're retaining 90% or less, something's off — usually the student is too small, the data is too narrow, or the loss weighting is wrong. Don't ship a student that's meaningfully worse than the teacher unless the latency budget genuinely has no room for the teacher; a slightly slower accurate model usually beats a fast wrong one.

Distillation underperforms in a few recognizable situations. When the teacher itself is barely above the data's inherent ceiling, there's little dark knowledge to transfer — a teacher at 99% accuracy on an easy task gives soft labels that are nearly hard, and the student learns little extra. When the student architecture is fundamentally mismatched to the task (e.g., a tiny model on a task requiring long-range reasoning), no amount of distillation recovers it. And when the deployment distribution differs from the training distribution, the student inherits the teacher's blind spots — distillation doesn't fix generalization gaps the teacher has.

The takeaway: distillation is the compression method that trades training compute for inference savings. You pay once in a distillation training run; you collect the inference savings every request for the life of the deployment. For a high-traffic endpoint, that's almost always the right trade. Pair it with [inference optimization](/blog/llm-inference-optimization) and quantization, and a model that was too expensive to ship becomes one you can run everywhere.

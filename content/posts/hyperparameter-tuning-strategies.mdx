Hyperparameter tuning is where ML projects quietly burn GPU budget. Run a naive grid search over five hyperparameters and you've scheduled hundreds of training runs, most of which explore regions you already knew were bad. The goal of a tuning strategy is to find good configurations with as little compute as possible — and to know when to stop. This guide covers the strategies that actually pay off, and how to scale them without waste.

## Tuning Approaches Compared

**Grid search** evaluates every combination of a fixed set of values. It's exhaustive and simple, but it scales catastrophically — 4 values across 5 parameters means 1024 runs — and it wastes effort exploring uninteresting dimensions uniformly. Use it only when you have a tiny search space and cheap runs.

**Random search** samples configurations at random from the space. The empirical result that made it famous: for most problems, only a few hyperparameters matter much, and random search explores the important ones far more efficiently than grid, which wastes samples on unimportant ones. It's the baseline you should beat.

**Bayesian optimization** (e.g. Gaussian processes or TPE) builds a probabilistic model of the objective as it goes, and uses it to choose the next configuration likely to improve on the best so far. It converges to good configurations in fewer trials than random search, especially when each trial is expensive. The tradeoff is that it's sequential by nature — parallelizing it weakens the model's ability to learn from results.

The practical recommendation: start with random search to get a baseline and rough landscape, then switch to Bayesian for the expensive final refinement. For deep learning specifically, Bayesian (TPE) over a well-chosen space is usually the right default.

## Search Space Design

The single most impactful hyperparameter is the learning rate. Search it on a log scale (e.g. 1e-5 to 1e-2), not linearly. Most other continuous hyperparameters (weight decay, dropout) also benefit from log-scale sampling where appropriate.

Batch size interacts with learning rate — when you scale batch size up, you usually need to scale the learning rate too (the linear scaling rule, with warmup). Don't tune them independently as if they were unrelated.

Use **pruning** to kill unpromising runs early. Median-stopping and successive halving terminate runs whose intermediate performance falls below the median of completed runs at the same step. This lets you start many configurations and concentrate compute on the ones that deserve it. Libraries like Ray Tune and Optuna support this natively.

<Callout type="tip">
Define your search space narrowly around what actually matters. A huge space with 10 parameters is harder to search and rarely better than a tight space on the 3–4 parameters that drive your metric. Domain knowledge beats exhaustive search.
</Callout>

## Scaling Sweeps

For serious tuning you want parallel trials. **Ray Tune** and **Optuna** are the two main frameworks. Ray Tune excels at distributed scheduling across a cluster and integrates well with PyTorch; Optuna is lighter and has excellent TPE pruning. Both support async trials.

The key tension: Bayesian optimization learns best from sequential results, but parallel trials are how you use a cluster efficiently. A common compromise is a "constant liar" approach — schedule several trials in parallel, then update the surrogate with all their results before the next batch. This keeps utilization high while still letting the model learn.

Budget-aware scheduling matters when GPU time is the constraint. Set a total trial budget, use pruning aggressively, and prioritize finishing cheap-but-informative runs before committing to long ones. Track every trial in your [experiment tracker](/blog/experiment-tracking-mlflow-vs-wandb) so you never lose a result.

## Reading the Results

Tuning can overfit to your validation set just like training overfits to training data. If you run hundreds of trials against a fixed validation split, the "best" configuration is partly the one that happened to do well on that specific data. Hold out a separate test set that you never tune against, and report final numbers there.

Look for statistical significance, not just the top score. If the top 10 configurations all have similar metrics, you've found a plateau — further tuning is unlikely to help and you should look elsewhere (more data, a different architecture). If results are noisy and scattered, your tuning budget is too small or your evaluation is too high-variance.

Finally, keep a reproducibility checklist: the exact search space, the trial count, the seed, and the framework version. A tuning run you can't reproduce is a tuning run you can't trust. Combined with the training optimizations in our [PyTorch speed guide](/blog/optimize-pytorch-training-speed), disciplined tuning turns raw GPU hours into actually better models instead of just more experiments.

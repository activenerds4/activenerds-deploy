I used an under-desk treadmill (walking pad) for three months while coding. Here's what actually happened — the good, the bad, and whether it's worth it for developers.

## The Walking-While-Working Hypothesis

The idea is simple: instead of standing still at your [standing desk](/articles/fitness-tracker-standing-desk-users), walk slowly while you work. Research suggests that slow walking (1-2 mph) during cognitive tasks doesn't impair performance and adds significant daily step count without requiring dedicated exercise time.

For developers who sit 8+ hours a day, the appeal is obvious: turn some of those hours into low-intensity movement without sacrificing productivity.

## What I Tested

- **Device**: WalkingPad A1 Pro (slim, foldable walking pad)
- **Testing period**: 3 months of daily use
- **Daily routine**: 2-3 hours of walking spread throughout the day at 1.5-2 mph
- **Workload**: Coding, code review, writing, and video calls

## Pros After 3 Months

### Step Count Increase
My average daily step count went from ~4,000 to ~12,000 without any dedicated walking time. That's the equivalent of a 45-minute walk, spread across the workday.

### Energy Levels
The afternoon energy crash I used to hit at 3pm mostly disappeared. The continuous low-level movement keeps blood flowing and prevents the lethargy that comes from prolonged sitting or standing.

### Back Pain Reduction
This was the biggest surprise. My lower back pain — which I'd attributed to "getting older" — decreased significantly. The gentle walking motion seems to keep the hip flexors and lower back from stiffening up the way they do when standing or sitting still.

## Cons and Limitations

### Typing Accuracy at Speed
At 1.5 mph, typing is fine. At 2 mph, I notice a slight decrease in typing accuracy — maybe 5% more errors. At 2.5+ mph, it becomes noticeable enough to be annoying during coding. The sweet spot for coding is 1-1.5 mph.

### Noise Level
The WalkingPad is quiet (under 60dB), but not silent. On video calls, people could hear a faint hum. It wasn't a problem, but it's worth knowing. If you're on calls all day, you'll probably want to step off during them.

### Space Requirements
You need clearance behind your desk for the belt, and the pad itself is about 5 feet long. In a small apartment, this is a real consideration. The foldable models help — I slide mine under the couch when not in use.

### Not for Deep Debugging
Walking is great for routine coding, email, and code review. For complex debugging or architecture work where I need to be fully focused, I found myself stopping the treadmill. Your mileage may vary — some people find walking helps them think.

<Callout type="tip">
Start at 0.5 mph and increase gradually. The first week feels awkward; by week two it becomes second nature. Don't judge it on day one.
</Callout>

## Verdict and Recommendations

**Who should get one**: Developers who already have a standing desk, want to increase daily movement, and do a mix of routine and deep-focus work. If you're currently fully sedentary, a standing desk alone is a better first step.

**Best speed for coding**: 1-1.5 mph. Fast enough to get steps, slow enough to type accurately.

**Alternatives**: If a walking pad is too much, consider a balance board or anti-fatigue mat for your standing desk. They add movement without the walking motion.

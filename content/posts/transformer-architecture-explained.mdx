The transformer is the architecture behind essentially every modern language model, and the reason it won is structural rather than magical: it removed the sequential bottleneck that limited RNNs, which let it scale with compute and data in a way earlier models couldn't. This is a practical walkthrough of the pieces and why they fit together, without drowning in the linear algebra. The goal is that after reading this, the architecture diagram stops looking like a pile of boxes and starts making sense as a sequence of deliberate design choices.

## The Problem Transformers Solved

Before transformers, sequence models were recurrent: an RNN processed a sequence one token at a time, carrying a hidden state forward. That had two consequences. First, computation was `O(sequence_length)` and couldn't be parallelized across the time dimension — training a long sequence meant waiting for every step. Second, information from early in the sequence had to survive a long chain of updates to reach later tokens, which it often didn't, producing the "vanishing gradient" and long-range-dependency problems everyone fought for years.

The transformer's key move was to drop recurrence entirely. Instead of processing tokens one at a time, it processes all positions simultaneously and lets each token directly attend to every other token in a single operation. That makes the per-layer computation parallelizable across the sequence, which is exactly what GPUs are good at — and it's the reason transformers scaled where RNNs plateaued. The cost is `O(n²)` attention, which is its own scaling problem, but one that's been more tractable than recurrence.

## Self-Attention Intuition

The core operation is self-attention. For each token, you want to compute a representation that's a weighted blend of every other token's representation, where the weights reflect how relevant each other token is. Concretely, each token emits three vectors — a query, a key, and a value. The query asks "what am I looking for?", the key answers "what do I contain?", and the value is what gets passed along if there's a match.

The actual computation: take dot products of the query with every key to get similarity scores, scale them down (by `√d_k`), softmax them into weights, and use those weights to sum the values. That gives each token a new representation that's already informed by the whole sequence. No recurrence, no long chains — every token sees every other token in one step.

Multi-head attention runs this process several times in parallel with different learned projections, then concatenates the results. The intuition is that different heads can specialize in different relations — one might track syntactic dependencies, another coreference, another positional patterns. You don't hand-design these; you just give the model multiple heads and let it learn the specialization. In practice, many heads end up redundant, but the redundancy is cheap and the useful heads more than pay for it.

## Position and Structure

Attention as described above is permutation-equivariant: it has no notion of token order. Feed the same tokens in a different order and you get the same attention weights, just permuted. That's a problem for language, where order matters, so transformers inject positional information explicitly. The original paper used fixed sinusoidal positional encodings added to the token embeddings; modern models more often use learned or relative positional encodings, or rotary (RoPE) encodings that rotate the query/key vectors by a position-dependent angle. The mechanism varies, but the goal is the same: tell the model where each token sits.

Two structural details matter operationally. **Residual connections** around each sub-layer let gradients flow through deep stacks and let each layer refine rather than overwrite — this is why you can stack dozens of layers without training falling apart. **Layer normalization** stabilizes the activations; the placement (pre-norm vs post-norm) turns out to matter a lot for training stability in deep models, and most modern implementations use pre-norm.

The encoder produces context-aware representations of the input; the decoder is autoregressive and uses masked self-attention (so position `i` can't see future positions) plus cross-attention into the encoder output. Not every model uses both: encoder-only models (BERT) are great for understanding tasks, decoder-only models (GPT) for generation, and encoder-decoder models (original Transformer, T5) for sequence-to-sequence tasks. The LLMs you use daily are almost all decoder-only.

## Why It Scales

The reason transformers dominate is less the [attention mechanism](/articles/attention-mechanism-explained) itself and more that the whole architecture is unusually friendly to scaling. Because there's no recurrence, you can throw more compute at a fixed sequence in parallel; because of the residual structure, you can stack more layers; and because attention gives every token direct access to every other, longer-range dependencies don't degrade with depth the way they did in RNNs.

Scaling laws describe the payoff: loss improves as a predictable power law in model size, data, and compute, which is what made the "train it bigger" strategy rational rather than a gamble. The `O(n²)` attention cost is the real ceiling — it's why context lengths were historically limited and why efficient-attention variants (sparse attention, sliding-window, flash attention as a memory optimization) are an active area. But the architectural core — parallel, residual, attention-based — is why we're in the era we're in, and it's worth understanding on its own terms rather than as a black box.

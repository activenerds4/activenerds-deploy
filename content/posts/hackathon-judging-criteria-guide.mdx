Hackathons have a strange dynamic: teams spend 24–48 hours building something impressive, then have three to five minutes to convince judges it's worth winning, and the judges — who've seen 30 projects that day — make a decision that feels arbitrary. It's not arbitrary, but it is subjective, and understanding how judging actually works is the difference between a project that's technically strong but loses and one that's technically strong and wins. This is a guide to what judges score, how they score it, and how to optimize your presentation without gaming the system.

## How Judging Actually Works

The mechanics are more structured than they appear. Most hackathons use a **rubric** — a set of scoring dimensions, each weighted, that judges fill out per project. The rubric is usually published in advance, and reading it is the single highest-leverage thing you can do before the event, because it tells you exactly what to optimize. A project that's brilliant on a dimension worth 10% of the score and weak on one worth 40% will lose to a project that's solid across the board. Optimize to the rubric, not to your own sense of what's impressive.

**Time per project** is the brutal constraint. Judges typically have 3–5 minutes per demo, often with a couple minutes of Q&A. In that window they're forming an impression, checking the rubric dimensions, and comparing against the 20 other projects they've seen. Your job in the demo is to make it easy for them to score you well on each dimension — to make the technical difficulty obvious, the impact clear, the polish visible. A demo that buries the lede makes the judge work to find your strengths, and tired judges don't work; they default to the impression.

**Subjectivity is real** and worth accepting. Two judges can score the same project differently based on their background — a technical judge may weight difficulty heavily, a design judge may weight polish. The rubric constrains this but doesn't eliminate it. The implication is that you should optimize for the median judge, not the specialist: make your strengths legible to someone who isn't an expert in your specific stack, because at least one judge probably isn't.

## Common Scoring Dimensions

Most rubrics share a common set of dimensions, even if the weights vary. Knowing these lets you structure your build and your demo around them.

**Originality and impact** — is the idea new, and does it solve a real problem? This is often the highest-weighted dimension and the one teams under-invest in. A technically impressive clone of an existing product scores poorly here; a simpler project that addresses a genuine, well-articulated problem scores well. The mistake is building the cool thing rather than the useful thing. Spend the first hour of the hackathon on the problem, not the stack.

**Technical difficulty** — how hard was this to build in the time available? Judges look for non-trivial integration, novel algorithms, or systems work. The challenge is making difficulty legible: a judge watching a demo can't see the clever backend architecture unless you show it. Mention the hard parts explicitly — "we're doing real-time inference with a model we quantized to fit the latency budget" tells a technical judge what to credit you for. Don't brag, but don't hide the work.

**Execution and polish** — does it work, and does it feel finished? A working, good-looking demo beats a more ambitious project that crashes. Polish is disproportionately rewarded because it's the most visible dimension and the one judges can assess instantly. A coherent design, no obvious bugs, and a smooth demo flow signal competence and make the judge trust the rest of your claims. This is where the last two hours of a hackathon should go — not new features, but making what you have work cleanly.

Some rubrics add **presentation** and **theme fit** as separate dimensions; treat them as part of the same picture. A demo that tells a clear story, addresses the event's theme, and lands its key points in the time limit scores well across all of them.

## Optimizing Without Gaming

There's a line between optimizing for the rubric and gaming it, and crossing it tends to backfire. The legitimate optimizations are about **clarity**, not deception.

**The demo is the deliverable.** Whatever you built, the judges see the demo, not the codebase. Structure the demo to hit each rubric dimension explicitly: open with the problem (impact), show the hard part working (difficulty), show the polished interface (execution), in 3–4 minutes. Rehearse the demo out loud at least twice — the first time you say it out loud you'll find it's 6 minutes and you need to cut. A demo that's been rehearsed is visibly better than one that hasn't, and it's free to do.

**Story over features** — resist the urge to enumerate everything you built. Judges remember a narrative ("we noticed X problem, so we built Y, and here's it working") over a feature list. One clear story, well-told, with one or two memorable moments, beats a comprehensive walkthrough that leaves no impression. Cut features from the demo ruthlessly; you can mention others in Q&A if asked.

**Handling judge questions** is where strong projects distinguish themselves. Judges ask to probe difficulty ("how did you handle X?") and to check if you actually built it ("did you use a library for this?"). Honest, specific answers build credibility; vague or defensive answers lose it. If you used a library, say so and explain what you built on top — judges respect honesty and penalize implied claims of building everything from scratch. If you don't know an answer, say how you'd find out; pretending you know when you don't is worse than admitting you don't.

## What Wins Beyond Code

The dimension that separates finalists from winners is often non-technical. **Team and presentation** — a team that demoed well, answered questions confidently, and clearly worked well together signals a project that could become real. Judges unconsciously weight this because it predicts follow-through, and follow-through is what hackathons are really trying to encourage.

**Addressing the theme** — if the hackathon has a theme (sustainability, AI for good, developer tools), a project that engages it thoughtfully scores well on theme-fit dimensions and stands out from the generic entries. A project that ignores the theme to build something cool-but-off-topic often loses to a less ambitious project that nailed the theme. Read the theme and take it seriously.

**Post-hackathon follow-through** is the long game. Many hackathons offer prizes beyond the event — incubation, funding, introductions — and judges and organizers remember teams that continued their project after the weekend. The hackathon is a starting point, not an end.

The teams that win the most over time are the ones who treat it that way: build something real, present it well, and keep going after the demo ends. The judging criteria reward the project in front of them, but the ecosystem rewards the project that continues. Optimize for both, and you'll do well by any measure.

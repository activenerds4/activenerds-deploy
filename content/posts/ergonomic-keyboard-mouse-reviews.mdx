Wrist pain is the most common injury among programmers, and your keyboard and mouse are the primary culprits. Standard keyboards force your wrists into pronation (palms down), and standard mice require repetitive micro-movements that strain tendons over time. This review covers the ergonomic input devices that actually help — based on months of testing, not marketing claims.

## Why Ergonomic Input Matters

### RSI Prevention

[Repetitive Strain](/articles/ergonomic-mouse-wrist-pain-relief) Injury (RSI) accounts for 60% of all workplace injuries in tech. The most common forms for programmers:

- **Carpal tunnel syndrome**: Compression of the median nerve in the wrist
- **Tendonitis**: Inflammation of tendons from repetitive motion
- **De Quervain's tenosynovitis**: Thumb tendon inflammation from mouse use
- **Tennis elbow (lateral epicondylitis)**: Forearm tendon inflammation

### Wrist Angle Research

Research from the Human Factors and Ergonomics Society shows that:
- **Pronation** (palms flat) increases pressure in the carpal tunnel by 30%
- **Ulnar deviation** (bending wrists outward) increases pressure by 50%
- **Neutral wrist position** (handshake position) minimizes carpal tunnel pressure

Ergonomic keyboards and mice are designed to bring your wrists closer to neutral.

### Productivity Impact

Pain doesn't just hurt — it slows you down. A study of software developers found that those with wrist pain typed 23% slower and made 15% more errors. Preventing RSI isn't just about health; it's about maintaining your output.

## Split Keyboards

### Kinesis Advantage360

The Advantage360 is the gold standard for split keyboards. It splits the keyboard into two halves, allowing each hand to type at shoulder width with a neutral wrist angle.

**Key features:**
- Concave key wells that reduce finger travel distance
- Fully programmable (QMK firmware)
- Wireless (Bluetooth) or wired
- Cherry MX switches (your choice of color)
- Tented design (keys tilt inward for neutral wrist)

**Pros:**
- Best wrist angle of any keyboard tested
- Concave wells reduce finger extension
- Fully programmable for custom layouts
- Available in wireless

**Cons:**
- Price: $399-$499
- Learning curve: 2-4 weeks to return to normal speed
- Large footprint on desk
- Not portable

### ErgoDox EZ

The ErgoDox EZ is a fully split, ortholinear keyboard. Unlike the Kinesis, it's flat (no concave wells) but offers more customization.

**Key features:**
- Fully split halves (adjustable distance)
- Ortholinear layout (keys in a grid, not staggered)
- Fully programmable (QMK)
- Tenting kit available
- Multiple switch options

**Pros:**
- Extremely customizable
- Ortholinear layout reduces finger stretch
- Good build quality
- Available with tenting

**Cons:**
- Price: $270-$370
- Ortholinear layout requires relearning
- No concave key wells
- Heavier than expected

### Learning Curve

<Callout type="info">
Expect a 2-4 week adjustment period when switching to a split keyboard. Your typing speed will drop initially (sometimes to 50% of normal) but should recover and often exceed your previous speed after 3-4 weeks. Practice with typing tests for 15 minutes daily during the transition.
</Callout>

## Vertical and Trackball Mice

### Logitech MX Vertical

The MX Vertical is the most popular vertical mouse. It rotates your hand 57 degrees into a "handshake" position, reducing wrist pronation.

**Key features:**
- 57-degree vertical angle
- 4000 DPI sensor
- USB-C fast charging (1 minute = 2 hours)
- Multi-device (connect to 3 devices)
- Customizable buttons via Logitech Options

**Pros:**
- Significant wrist comfort improvement
- Good build quality
- Long battery life (4 months per charge)
- Multi-device switching

**Cons:**
- Price: $99
- Takes 1-2 weeks to adjust
- Larger than standard mouse
- Not ideal for gaming

### Logitech MX Ergo Trackball

The MX Ergo is a trackball mouse — the ball moves, not the mouse. This eliminates arm movement entirely, which helps with shoulder and elbow issues.

**Key features:**
- Thumb-controlled trackball
- Adjustable tilt (0-20 degrees)
- 512 DPI trackball sensor
- USB-C charging
- Multi-device (2 devices)

**Pros:**
- Zero arm movement (good for shoulder pain)
- Adjustable tilt for wrist comfort
- Long battery life (4 months)
- Precise cursor control

**Cons:**
- Price: $99
- Thumb strain possible (different RSI risk)
- Learning curve for trackball
- Not suitable for fast-paced work

### Logitech Trackball (M575)

A budget trackball option at $59:

## Transition Tips

When switching to ergonomic input devices:

1. **Start gradually**: Use the new device for 1-2 hours per day, increasing over 2 weeks
2. **Keep the old one handy**: Switch back if you have a deadline and need full speed
3. **Practice typing**: 15 minutes of typing tests daily during transition
4. **Adjust your [desk height](/articles/ergonomic-desk-setup-programmers)**: Ergonomic devices work best with proper desk height
5. **Take breaks**: During the transition, take more frequent micro-breaks

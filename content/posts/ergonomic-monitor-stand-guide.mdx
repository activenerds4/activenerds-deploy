The monitor is the part of your desk you stare at for eight hours, and the height it sits at determines whether your neck is happy or quietly accumulating strain for the next decade. Most monitors sit too low on their stock stands, which puts your head tilted down — a position that adds the equivalent of a 20–30 pound load to your neck and is the single most common cause of the sore-neck-and-tight-shoulders complaint among developers. A monitor stand or arm fixes this for less than the cost of a single physio session. This is a guide to choosing one and setting it up right.

## Why Monitor Height Matters

**Neck strain from low screens.** The biomechanics are straightforward: your head weighs about 10–12 pounds in a neutral position, and every inch it tilts forward roughly doubles the effective load on your neck muscles. Looking down at a screen on a stock stand — typically 4–6 inches below eye level — leaves your neck holding 25+ pounds for hours, which is why "my neck hurts" is the universal desk-worker complaint. The fix is mechanical: raise the screen so the top of the viewable area is at or just below eye level, and your neck returns to neutral.

**The ideal eye line.** The target is the top edge of the screen at eye level when you're sitting (or standing) with good posture, with the screen an arm's length away. Your eyes then naturally look down about 15–20 degrees to the center of the screen, which is the resting gaze angle — the position where your eye muscles and neck are both relaxed. A screen too high forces you to tilt your head back (also bad); too low forces the forward head posture. The top-at-eye-level rule gets you to the relaxed middle.

**Depth and focal distance.** Distance matters as much as height. A monitor too close forces your eyes to converge hard and is tiring; too far and you lean in. An arm's length (roughly 20–30 inches from your eyes to the screen) is the standard, adjusted for screen size — bigger screens go a bit farther. If you can't read text comfortably at arm's length, the fix is larger text or glasses, not moving the screen closer.

## Stands vs Arms

**Fixed stands limitations.** The stand that comes with a monitor is built to a price and to fit in a box. It adjusts for tilt, sometimes for height, almost never for depth, and the height range is usually a few inches — not enough to get from "on the desk" to "eye level" for most people. Riser blocks and books are the common workaround, and they work, but they're fiddly and they don't help with depth or with a [standing desk](/articles/monitor-arm-mount-standing-desk) where the geometry changes when you stand.

**Single vs dual arms.** A monitor arm clamps to the desk and holds the monitor on a articulated arm, giving full height, depth, tilt, and rotation adjustment. For a single monitor, a single arm is the upgrade. For dual monitors, you can use two single arms or one dual-arm on a shared post — the shared post is more stable but less flexible, two singles are more adjustable but need more desk edge. For a standing desk, an arm is especially valuable because you can reposition the monitor when you change posture, which a fixed stand can't do. See our [monitor arm mount comparison](/blog/monitor-arm-mount-standing-desk) for the standing-desk-specific considerations.

**Desk clamp vs grommet.** Arms attach either by clamping to the desk edge or by mounting through a grommet hole (the cable-pass-through hole some desks have). Clamps are universal and don't require drilling; grommet mounts are more stable for heavy monitors and free up desk edge, but need a desk with a grommet or a willingness to drill. For most people on a standard desk, a clamp is the right choice.

## Setup and Adjustment

**Height and tilt.** Set the height so the top of the screen is at eye level when you're in your normal working posture — sit back, shoulders relaxed, not leaning in. Then tilt the screen back about 10–15 degrees so it's square to your gaze; untilted, a flat screen at eye level shows you a foreshortened view that's subtly harder to read. Re-check this when you switch between sitting and standing on a height-adjustable desk, because eye level moves with your posture.

**Cable management.** A monitor on an arm moves freely, which means cables need slack or they pull the monitor back to a position you didn't choose. Route the power and video cables through the arm's cable channel if it has one, and leave enough slack for the full range of motion. A cable that's taut at one end of the arm's range will fight you and slowly drag the monitor out of position.

**Stability with heavy monitors.** The failure mode of cheap arms is bounce — you type, the monitor wobbles. Heavier monitors (27"+ or any ultrawide) need an arm rated for the weight, and a clamp that grips well, or the monitor will oscillate for a second after every adjustment. The spec to check is the weight range; buy an arm whose max weight is comfortably above your monitor's actual weight, not one that's maxed out by your monitor.

## Buying Guidance

**Weight capacity matters.** This is the single most important spec. An arm rated for 4–8 lbs will wobble with a 10 lb monitor; an arm rated for 15–20 lbs will hold a typical 27–32" monitor rock-steady. Ultrawides and older IPS panels are heavier than you'd guess. Check the VESA compatibility (75x75 or 100x100 covers almost everything) and the weight, in that order.

**Budget vs premium.** Budget arms ($40–80) work for lighter monitors and occasional adjustment, but the springs are weaker, the adjustment is stiffer, and they wobble more. Premium arms ($150–300) have gas-strut adjustment that holds position without tightening a knob, better cable management, and stability that doesn't degrade. For a monitor you adjust daily on a sit-stand desk, the premium arm pays for itself in not-fighting-your-gear; for a fixed-height desk with a single monitor you set once, a budget arm is fine.

**Our picks.** The right choice depends on your setup. For a single monitor on a fixed desk, any VESA arm with the right weight rating works, and a budget model is a reasonable choice. For a standing desk where you reposition the monitor daily, spend for a gas-strut arm — the difference in daily friction is worth it. For dual monitors, two single arms give more flexibility than a shared-post dual arm unless desk edge space is tight. Whatever you choose, the upgrade from a stock stand to any arm is the single highest-value ergonomic change you can make for under $150, and it directly addresses the most common cause of desk-worker neck pain — see our broader [ergonomic desk setup guide](/blog/ergonomic-desk-setup-programmers) for how it fits with chair, keyboard, and mouse.

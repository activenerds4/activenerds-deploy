# On-Call Survival & Circadian Recovery Guide

**An on-call rotation isn't a sprint — it's a tour of duty.** The pager doesn't care that you were in deep sleep, that it's 3 AM, or that you've already been woken twice tonight. What separates engineers who last in on-call from those who burn out isn't toughness — it's protocol. This guide covers the incident triage sequence, the physiological cost of disrupted sleep, and the recovery playbook that turns a brutal week back into a sustainable career.

## 1. The Physiology of On-Call

Before we get to protocols, understand what the pager actually does to your body. The 2 AM alert doesn't just wake you — it triggers a cascade of stress hormones that persist long after the incident is resolved.

### The Cortisol Awakening Response

Normally, cortisol rises 30–45 minutes before your habitual wake time, preparing your body for the day. An unexpected 2 AM alert hijacks this system:

1. **Adrenaline surge** — immediate, within seconds. Heart rate spikes, blood pressure rises.
2. **Cortisol spike** — follows within minutes. Triggers glucose release, suppresses non-essential functions (including the immune system).
3. **Sleep architecture disruption** — even after you fall back asleep, REM and deep sleep are suppressed. The "asleep but not resting" state.

<Callout variant="warning" title="The 'I slept 4 hours' math error">
  Getting woken at 2 AM, resolving an incident in 45 minutes, and falling back asleep doesn't mean you got 4 hours of additional sleep. The stress hormones in your bloodstream prevent restorative sleep for 1–2 hours after the incident. A 45-minute interruption can cost you 2–3 hours of effective rest. Plan recovery accordingly.
</Callout>

### Sleep Debt Accumulation

A single bad night is survivable. The danger is compounding:

| Nights of disrupted sleep | Cognitive impact | Recovery time |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Mild reaction-time impairment | 1 good night |
| 2 | Noticeable focus reduction, irritability | 2–3 nights |
| 3 | Equivalent to legal alcohol impairment | 3–5 nights |
| 5+ | Burnout territory, decision-making errors | 1+ week |

This is why a week of heavy on-call needs active recovery, not just "sleeping in on Saturday."

## 2. Pre-Shift Preparation

The best time to handle an incident is before it happens. Set up your environment, your tooling, and your body for rapid response.

### The On-Call Ready Kit

<ChecklistBox
  title="Pre-Shift Setup"
  items={[
    "Laptop within arm's reach of bed, charged and connected to VPN",
    "Runbooks bookmarked and tested — not 'I'll figure it out at 3 AM'",
    "On-call phone fully charged, ringer volume at maximum",
    "Slack/PagerDuty app notifications set to override Do Not Disturb",
    "Personal phone on silent, work phone is the only audible one",
    "Water and a low-sugar snack within reach for the post-incident recovery window",
    "Room at sleep-friendly temperature (18–20°C) — not too warm, which fragments sleep",
    "Blackout curtains or eye mask in place — darkness preserves melatonin",
  ]}
/>

### The Body Pre-Load

The night before an on-call shift:

- **Stop caffeine by 2 PM** — caffeine has a 5–6 hour half-life; a 2 PM coffee still has 25% of its peak concentration at 8 PM
- **Last meal 2–3 hours before bed** — digestion raises core body temperature, which delays sleep onset
- **Bias lighting on** — dim, warm (2700K) light in your bedroom; this is the inverse of the blue-light problem we'll address in section 4
- **Alcohol-free evening** — alcohol suppresses REM sleep and worsens the sleep fragmentation from being woken

<Callout variant="tip" title="Train your body to the rotation">
  If your rotation is weekly, try to maintain a consistent sleep window even on non-on-call days. A body that goes to bed at 11 PM and wakes at 7 AM every day handles a 2 AM interruption far better than a body on an irregular schedule. Circadian consistency is leverage.
</Callout>

## 3. 2 AM Incident Triage Protocol

When the pager goes off, the goal isn't to solve the problem perfectly — it's to stabilize fast and preserve your ability to go back to sleep.

### The 4-Step Triage Sequence

```text
STEP 1 — ACKNOWLEDGE  (60 seconds)
  - Acknowledge the page in PagerDuty
  - Open the relevant dashboard on your phone
  - Silence the audible alert on your phone

STEP 2 — CLASSIFY     (2 minutes)
  - Severity: Sev1 (revenue down) vs Sev2 (degraded) vs Sev3 (nuisance)
  - Is this a known issue with an existing runbook?
  - Is another responder already on it?

STEP 3 — ACT          (variable)
  - Sev3: Can this wait until morning? If yes, snooze and go back to sleep.
  - Sev2: Can a mitigation buy time? (Scale up, fail over, rollback.)
  - Sev1: Full mobilization. Wake a second responder. It's not a solo job.

STEP 4 — DOCUMENT      (5 minutes, post-stabilization)
  - Incident channel created, stakeholders notified
  - Timeline of what happened and what you did
  - Decision: resolved, mitigated, or escalating
```

### The Wake-Up Decision Framework

Not every page requires you to fully wake up. The goal is to match your level of activation to the incident's actual severity.

| Severity | Your state | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Sev3 (noise) | Half-awake | Acknowledge, snooze 2 hours, go back to sleep |
| Sev2 (degraded) | Alert but horizontal | Mitigate from phone, escalate if can't |
| Sev1 (down) | Fully up | Get to laptop, mobilize team, this is a real incident |

<Callout variant="warning" title="Don't 'just quickly check' at 3 AM">
  The worst on-call decision is the 3 AM "quick check" that turns into a 90-minute debugging session you weren't planning to start. If it's not a Sev1, triage it as such: acknowledge, assess whether it can wait until 7 AM, and if yes, go back to sleep. You'll resolve it in 20 minutes in the morning what would take 90 minutes at 3 AM.
</Callout>

## 4. Blue-Light & Cortisol Mitigation

The phone screen at 2 AM is a circadian weapon. Blue light (460–480 nm wavelength) suppresses melatonin and signals your brain that it's daytime — the opposite of what you need at 3 AM.

### The Blue-Light Mitigation Stack

1. **Phone: Night Shift / blue-light filter ON, at maximum** — this is non-negotiable for on-call nights
2. **Screen brightness: minimum viable** — dark mode, lowest brightness that lets you read the dashboard
3. **Room light: off or red-only** — if you must have light, use a red filter or red flashlight; red light doesn't suppress melatonin
4. **Avoid overhead lights** — any bright, broad-spectrum light will reset your circadian phase

### The Cortisol Flush

After the incident is resolved, your body is still loaded with stress hormones. You can't will yourself back to sleep — you have to actively downregulate.

| Technique | Duration | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Box breathing (4-4-4-4) | 5 minutes | Activates parasympathetic nervous system |
| Cold water on face | 30 seconds | Mammalian dive reflex slows heart rate |
| Progressive muscle relaxation | 10 minutes | Releases residual muscle tension |
| No re-engagement with Slack | until morning | Prevents re-triggering the stress response |

<Callout variant="tip" title="The 20-minute rule for going back to sleep">
  If you can't fall back asleep within 20 minutes, get out of bed. Lying awake trains your brain to associate the bed with wakefulness. Go to a different room, do a low-stimulation activity (read a physical book, stretch), and return to bed only when you feel sleepy. Don't look at the clock — clock-watching increases sleep anxiety.
</Callout>

## 5. Post-Incident Recovery

The incident is resolved. The dashboard is green. But you're not done — now begins the recovery phase, which most on-call engineers skip entirely.

### The Morning-After Protocol

After a night with a significant incident:

<ChecklistBox
  title="Post-Incident Morning Recovery"
  items={[
    "Hydrate aggressively — stress hormones are dehydrating; aim for 1L of water on waking",
    "Skip the gym for heavy lifts — your cortisol is already elevated; do light cardio or mobility instead",
    "Caffeine delay — wait 60–90 minutes after waking before your first coffee to let natural cortisol clear",
    "Sunlight exposure — 10 minutes of natural light within an hour of waking resets the circadian phase",
    "Light, protein-rich breakfast — avoid sugar crashes that compound the fatigue",
    "Nap window — if possible, a 20–30 minute nap between 1–3 PM (not later)",
    "No alcohol that evening — it will suppress the REM sleep you need to recover",
    "Lights out 30–60 minutes earlier than normal to extend the recovery sleep window",
  ]}
/>

### The Multi-Incident Week

If you've been woken 3+ nights in one on-call week, you're in sleep debt territory. The recovery math:

- **Debt:** ~2–3 hours of effective sleep lost per disrupted night
- **Repayment rate:** ~1 hour of extra sleep per night, not all at once
- **Recovery window:** A full week of on-call disruption needs 3–5 nights of extended sleep to fully repay

<Callout variant="warning" title="Watch for the 'I'm fine' trap">
  Sleep-deprived people are the worst judges of their own impairment. By the time you feel impaired, you've been impaired for days. If your rotation was heavy, assume you need more recovery than you think you do. Take the comp day. Take the long weekend. Your judgment during sleep debt is the thing most likely to cause the next incident.
</Callout>

## 6. Rotation Sustainability

On-call is sustainable if the rotation is designed well and you recover actively. It's a career-ender if it isn't. Here's how to make it last.

### Rotation Design Principles

| Principle | Target | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Rotation length | 1 week max, ideally shorter | Longer rotations accumulate compounding debt |
| Team size | 4+ people for 24/7 coverage | Fewer means no recovery buffer between shifts |
| Secondary escalation | Always defined, always reachable | Primary should never feel solo at 3 AM |
| Comp time | 1 day per heavy week | Active recovery, not a luxury |
| Post-incident rest | Mandatory 12 hours post-Sev1 | You're not decision-fit after an all-nighter |

### The Career-Arc View

On-call isn't meant to be a permanent state — it's a phase. The engineers who last are the ones who:

- Treat recovery as a non-negotiable part of the job, not optional
- Escalate when the rotation is unsustainable (staffing, tooling, runbooks)
- Build the runbooks that reduce their own future pages
- Advocate for Follow-the-Sun coverage when the team grows

<Callout variant="tip" title="On-call is leverage — invest in reducing it">
  Every runbook you write, every alert you tune, every dashboard you build is an investment in your own future sleep. The engineers who sleep best during on-call aren't the lucky ones — they're the ones who did the work to make their systems observable, their alerts actionable, and their runbooks complete. On-call pain is a signal to invest, not a test of toughness.
</Callout>

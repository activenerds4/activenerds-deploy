# The Ergonomic Workstation Engineering Guide

**Your workstation is infrastructure you deploy on your own body.** Get the geometry wrong for a decade and you'll feel it in your wrists, your neck, and your lower back — long after the framework you were debugging has been deprecated. This guide treats the desk like a system: measure the inputs, tune the outputs, and monitor for drift.

## 1. Display Focal Distance & Geometry

The single highest-leverage adjustment you can make is monitor placement. Get this wrong and every other investment — the standing desk, the ergonomic chair — is fighting an uphill battle.

### The Focal Distance Formula

Your eyes resolve text most comfortably at a distance where the on-screen characters subtend a comfortable visual angle without inducing accommodative spasm (eye muscle fatigue from sustained near-focus).

**Recommended focal distance:** 50 to 100 cm (20 to 40 inches) from the cornea to the screen surface.

A practical field test:

```text
Arm's-length rule — fully extend your arm.
Your fingertips should *just barely* touch the screen.
If you can reach past the bezel, the monitor is too close.
If you can't reach it at all, it may be too far for your focal length.
```

### Vertical Geometry

| Measurement | Target | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Top of screen | At or slightly below eye level | Prevents sustained neck extension |
| Center of screen | 10–15° below horizontal gaze line | Matches natural resting eye position |
| Dual monitors | Primary centered, secondary at 15–30° | Minimizes sustained cervical rotation |
| Tilt | 10–20° back from vertical | Reduces glare and matches downward gaze |

<Callout variant="warning" title="The 'too high' trap">
  Most developers set their monitor too high, not too low. The top of the screen should be at eye level — never above. If your monitor sits on top of a shelf or a stack of books, you're craning your neck upward for 8 hours a day. The top bezel aligns with your seated eye line; the rest falls below it.
</Callout>

### Lateral Offset

If you use a single monitor, center it directly in front of your keyboard. If dual, place the seam between the two monitors centered on your midline if you use both equally. If one is primary, center that one and angle the secondary inward.

**Measured cost of asymmetry:** Each 15° of sustained lateral rotation loads the cervical facets on one side. Over an 8-hour day that's an imbalance your levator scapulae will announce by Wednesday.

## 2. Sit-Stand Duty Cycles

Standing all day is not the opposite of sitting all day — it's the same problem wearing a different costume. Static loading is static loading, whether it's through your ischial tuberosities (sitting) or your lumbar facets and plantar fascia (standing).

### The Duty Cycle

The research converges on a ratio: **for every 1 hour standing, sit for 1–2 hours.** The sweet spot for most people is 20–40 minutes of standing per 60–90 minute block, cycled throughout the day.

| Time on Task | Posture | Cue |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:20 | Standing | Start block standing |
| 0:20–0:40 | Standing | Anti-fatigue mat engaged |
| 0:40–1:00 | Sitting | Transition, sit down |
| 1:00–1:30 | Sitting | Focused deep work |
| 1:30–1:40 | Standing or walking | Micro-break, refill water |
| 1:40–2:00 | Sitting | Resume focus block |

<Callout variant="tip" title="Don't stand still — fidget on purpose">
  Standing desks fail when people stand stock-still. Static standing loads the same muscles continuously. The fix is micromovement: shift weight side to side, do calf raises while reviewing a PR, stand on one leg while reading documentation. The goal is dynamic loading, not a second posture held rigidly.
</Callout>

### Transition Tolerance

If you're new to a standing desk, don't jump to 4 hours of standing on day one. Your calves, feet, and posterior chain need adaptation time.

| Week | Max standing per day | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 hour total, 15-min blocks | Expect calf and foot fatigue |
| 2 | 2 hours total, 20-min blocks | Start weight-shifting drills |
| 3 | 3 hours total, 30-min blocks | Add calf raises and fidgeting |
| 4+ | 3–4 hours total, 20–40-min cycles | Sustainable indefinitely |

## 3. Input Device Biomechanics

Your hands are the interface between your brain and the machine. They're also the most repetitive-motion-intensive part of a developer's body, which makes them the most injury-prone.

### Keyboard Height & Tilt

The keyboard should sit at or slightly below elbow height — never above. When your forearms are parallel to the floor and your elbows are at 90–100°, your wrists should be **neutral** (flat), not extended (bent upward).

| Input | Height | Tilt | Wrist Position |
|---|---|---|---|
| Keyboard | At or below elbow | Negative or flat | Neutral, straight |
| Mouse | Same level as keyboard | Flat | No shoulder reaching |
| Trackpad | Same level as keyboard | Flat | Neutral pronation |

### Mechanical Switch Ergonomics

Mechanical keyboards aren't just for tactile feedback — the switch type affects how much force your fingers apply and for how long.

- **Linear switches** (e.g., Red): Light actuation, low force — good for low-impact typists who want speed
- **Tactile switches** (e.g., Brown): Bump feedback at actuation — good for most developers; the tactile event helps you release contact force
- **Clicky switches** (e.g., Blue): Loud, high actuation force — avoid for long sessions; the click adds no ergonomic value and the higher force fatigues
- **Low-profile switches**: Shorter travel distance — less finger extension, easier for smaller hands

<Callout variant="note" title="The contact-force insight">
  The hidden ergonomic variable isn't the switch type — it's the force you apply *after* the key registers. Many typists keep pressing through the bottom-out force long after the character is sent. Tactile switches help because the bump signals "you're done, stop pushing." Train yourself to release contact force immediately after actuation.
</Callout>

### The Split Keyboard Question

Split keyboards (like the ErgoDox or Kyria) aren't just for wrist pain — they address shoulder internal rotation. A standard keyboard forces your forearms into pronation (palms down) and your shoulders into internal rotation. A split board lets you splay your hands to match your shoulder width, opening the chest and externally rotating the shoulders.

**Who benefits most:** Anyone with ulnar deviation (wrists bending outward toward pinkies), thoracic tension, or shoulder discomfort from narrow keyboards.

## 4. Neutral Posture Tuning

"Good posture" isn't a position you hold — it's a neutral alignment your body returns to between movements. The goal is to make neutral the path of least resistance, not to lock yourself into one rigid shape.

### The Spinal Stack

Your spine isn't a single column — it's a stack of curves that distribute load. Sitting or standing well means stacking those curves so each segment sits efficiently on the one below it.

```text
Cranium     — level, ears over shoulders
Cervical    — natural curve, chin tucked (not jutting)
Thoracic    — slight extension, shoulders back and down
Lumbar      — natural lordotic curve, supported
Pelvis      — neutral (not anteriorly tilted, not posteriorly tucked)
```

### The 90-90-90 Baseline

A neutral seated baseline:

- Hips at 90–100° (slightly open, not cramped)
- Knees at 90°, feet flat on floor (or footrest)
- Elbows at 90–100°, forearms parallel to floor
- Wrists neutral (not extended or flexed)

<Callout variant="warning" title="The 'sitting up straight' myth">
  Forcing a rigid 90° upright posture is not neutral — it's sustained muscle contraction. The research-supported alternative is the 135° relaxed reclined posture (135° between torso and thighs), which reduces disc pressure compared to upright sitting. The real goal: change positions frequently rather than hold any one "perfect" posture.
</Callout>

### Lumbar Support Calibration

Lumbar support is the most-misunderstood adjustment. The support pad should contact the inward curve of your lower back (L3–L5 region), not float in space behind your shoulder blades.

**Calibration steps:**

1. Sit all the way back in the chair
2. Find the deepest inward curve of your lower back
3. Adjust the lumbar pad height so it fills that curve
4. Set depth so it's supportive, not pushing you forward
5. Re-check after 30 minutes — if you've slid forward, the depth is too aggressive

## 5. Environmental Layer

Ergonomics isn't just your body and the desk — it's the room around you.

### Lighting

- **Bias lighting** behind the monitor reduces contrast between the bright screen and dark room, easing accommodative load
- **Ambient lux:** 300–500 lux for screen work — bright enough to read paper documents, not so bright it creates glare
- **Color temperature:** warmer (2700–3500K) in evening to minimize blue-light circadian disruption; cooler during the day is fine

### Temperature & Airflow

Most offices are 2–4°C too cold for sedentary work. Your metabolic rate is low when sitting, so you generate less heat. Layer up rather than raising the thermostat — your IT department won't let you anyway.

### Noise

Sustained noise above 55 dB measurably increases cortisol and impairs focus. If your environment is loud, noise-canceling headphones are an ergonomic intervention, not a luxury.

## 6. Maintenance & Drift Detection

Your body adapts to whatever you do most. If you sit perfectly for a month then gradually slump, you won't notice — until the pain arrives. Drift detection is the answer.

<ChecklistBox
  title="Weekly Drift Audit"
  items={[
    "Take a side-profile photo of your seated posture — compare to last week",
    "Check monitor height: is the top bezel still at eye level after furniture shifts?",
    "Verify keyboard hasn't crept forward (elbows should stay at 90–100°)",
    "Scan for unilateral tension: is one shoulder higher than the other?",
    "Test wrist neutrality: are your wrists flat or extended while typing?",
    "Confirm lumbar pad still contacts your lower back curve, not your mid-back",
    "Check foot position: flat on floor or footrest, not tucked under the chair",
  ]}
/>

### The Posture Reset

Every 20–30 minutes, run a 10-second reset:

1. Reach both arms overhead, interlace fingers, palms up — full spinal extension
2. Roll shoulders backward 5 times — thoracic mobility
3. Chin tuck 5 times — deep neck flexor activation
4. Stand up if you've been sitting — even 30 seconds of standing breaks the static load

<Callout variant="tip" title="Set it and forget it — then check the dashboard">
  The most ergonomic workstation in the world fails if you drift. Treat posture like a deploy: configure it, monitor it, and alert on drift. A weekly 30-second photo audit catches the gradual slouch before it becomes a six-month problem.
</Callout>

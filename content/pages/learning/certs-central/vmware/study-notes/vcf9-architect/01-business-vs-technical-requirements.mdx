## Objective 1.1: Business vs Technical Requirements


<AccordionGroup type="single">
<AccordionItem title="Deep-Dive: The RCAR Framework (Requirements, Constraints, Assumptions, Risks)">

The VMware Certified Design Expert (VCDX) and VCF Architect exams heavily test your ability to classify statements into the **RCAR Framework**:

* **Business Requirement:** High-level outcome the business demands (e.g., *"Reduce datacenter carbon footprint by 25%"* or *"Maintain 99.99% core banking availability"*). It **never** specifies products or protocols.
* **Technical Requirement:** Measurable technical capability derived from business requirements (e.g., *"RPO &le; 5 minutes and RTO &le; 15 minutes for Tier-1 database VMs"*).
* **Constraint:** Non-negotiable boundary imposed from outside the architecture team (e.g., *"Must reuse existing Cisco Nexus 9000 switches"* or *"All patient data must remain geographically inside Germany"*).
* **Assumption:** An unverified belief accepted as true (e.g., *"Existing dark fiber link has latency under 4ms RTT"*). **Every unverified assumption is a hidden risk.**
* **Risk:** A potential adverse event with a probability and impact score (e.g., *"Delay in delivery of ReadyNode hardware may slip deployment timeline by 6 weeks"*).

</AccordionItem>

</AccordionGroup>


| | Business Requirement | Technical Requirement |
|---|---|---|
| **Describes** | *What the organisation needs to achieve* (outcome, value) | *What the solution must do or provide* to satisfy the business need |
| **Written in** | Business language, measurable outcomes | Technical language, specific capabilities |
| **Source** | Executives, business owners, stakeholders | Architects/engineers, derived from business requirements |
| **Examples** | "Provision new dev environments in under 1 hour", "Meet PCI-DSS", "Reduce DC cost 20%", "99.99% service availability" | "Self-service catalogue via VCF Automation", "Micro-segmentation with NSX DFW", "vSAN FTT=2 for critical workloads", "Backup RPO ≤ 15 min" |

**Key takeaways**
- Business requirements **drive** technical requirements. Every technical requirement should trace back to a business requirement.
- Business requirements are **solution-agnostic**: no product names or technologies.
- Technical requirements are **testable and measurable** (e.g., RTO, RPO, latency, IOPS, capacity).
- If a statement names a product, protocol, or specification, it is almost never a business requirement.
- Architect's job: gather requirements, clarify ambiguity, resolve conflicts between stakeholders, then translate.

## Objective 1.2: Conceptual Model vs Logical Design vs Physical Design

| Level | Audience | Detail | Answers | VCF 9.0 example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Conceptual Model** | Business stakeholders, executives | Highest-level, vendor-neutral, no specifics | *What* are we building and why? | "A private cloud platform offering self-service compute, storage and networking with governance" |
| **Logical Design** | Architects, senior engineers | Components, relationships, rules, **no specific hardware or vendor config** | *How* will components fit together to meet requirements? | "Management domain plus a workload domain; clusters using software-defined storage; NSX overlay with T0/T1 tiering; central operations and automation" |
| **Physical Design** | Implementers, engineers | Exact products, versions, sizing, IPs, VLANs, hardware, configuration | *With what* exactly, and where? | "4× ReadyNode hosts, vSAN ESA, 25 GbE uplinks, MTU 9000, VLAN IDs, specific VCF 9.0 build" |

**Key takeaways**
- Progression: **Conceptual → Logical → Physical** (increasing detail, narrowing audience, decreasing abstraction).
- **Logical = vendor/hardware independent** (e.g., "shared storage cluster"). **Physical = named, sized and configured** (e.g., "vSAN ESA, 24 NVMe per host").
- Changing the physical layer (swap hardware vendor) should *not* require changing the logical design. That is the point of the separation.
- VCF's standardised building blocks (management domain, workload domains, fleet/instance model) let the logical design map to a **validated physical** pattern.
- Spotting trick: IP addresses, VLAN IDs, host counts, model numbers → **Physical**. Component roles and relationships → **Logical**. Vision and scope → **Conceptual**.

## Objective 1.3: Requirements, Assumptions, Constraints, Risks

| Element | Definition | Example (VCF 9.0 context) |
|---|---|---|
| **Requirement** | A need the design **must** satisfy | "Workloads must survive a single-host failure" |
| **Assumption** | Something **believed true but not verified**; accepted without proof | "Existing network team will deliver 25 GbE and required VLANs before deployment" |
| **Constraint** | A **limitation or restriction** on design choices, imposed externally (budget, policy, tech, regulation) | "Must use existing storage array" / "Hardware must be on the VMware compatibility list" / "Data must remain in-country" |
| **Risk** | A **potential event** that could negatively impact the project or design; has probability and impact | "If the network upgrade slips, deployment slips" |

**Key takeaways**
- **Constraints limit the solution; requirements shape it; assumptions must be validated; risks must be managed.**
- An unvalidated **assumption is a hidden risk**. Validate it (becomes fact or requirement) or record it as a risk.
- A constraint is **not negotiable by the design team** (it comes from outside); a requirement may be negotiated with stakeholders.
- Risks are tracked with likelihood, impact, owner and mitigation.
- Common exam trap: "Budget is capped at $X" is a **constraint**, not a requirement. "Team has no NSX skills" is a **constraint** (or risk if future training uncertain).

## Objective 1.4: AMPRS Framework

**AMPRS** = the five design quality attributes used to evaluate and justify design decisions.

| Quality | Meaning | Typical VCF 9.0 design considerations |
|---|---|---|
| **A**vailability | Service is up and accessible when needed (uptime, SLA, eliminating single points of failure) | Clustered management domain, vSphere HA, vSAN FTT/failure domains, NSX Edge/T0 redundancy, stretched cluster, redundant uplinks and power |
| **M**anageability | Ease of deploying, operating, monitoring, patching, scaling | Centralised lifecycle management and fleet management, consistent standardised domains, VCF Operations for monitoring/capacity, VCF Automation for self-service, naming and tagging standards |
| **P**erformance | Meeting throughput, latency, capacity and scalability targets | Sizing CPU/memory/storage, vSAN ESA, NIC speeds, MTU, NSX Edge sizing, DRS, avoiding oversubscription |
| **R**ecoverability | Ability to restore service and data after failure or disaster (RTO/RPO) | Backup of management components, replication/DR, immutable backups, runbook, recovery sequencing |
| **S**ecurity | Confidentiality, integrity, access control, compliance | NSX micro-segmentation, RBAC, identity federation/SSO, encryption (vSAN, vMotion), certificate management, hardening, logging/audit |

**Key takeaways**
- AMPRS is the **lens for evaluating every design decision**: each decision typically improves some qualities and **trades off** others.
- **Trade-offs are inevitable**: e.g., higher availability (more redundancy) = higher cost and more complexity (manageability); stronger security can reduce performance or manageability.
- **Cost is not one of the five qualities**; it is typically a **constraint** or a separate consideration.
- Availability ≠ Recoverability: **Availability prevents/limits outage; Recoverability restores after one** (RTO/RPO).
- When asked "which quality does this decision primarily impact?", map the *purpose* of the decision: redundancy → A; lifecycle/automation → M; sizing/speed → P; backup/DR → R; access/encryption → S.

## Objective 1.5: Risk Mitigation Strategies

| Strategy | What you do | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Avoid** | Change the design/plan so the risk **cannot occur** | Drop an unsupported hardware platform; don't deploy an unvalidated feature |
| **Mitigate (Reduce)** | Take action to **lower likelihood and/or impact** | Add redundant uplinks; run a proof of concept; train staff; phased rollout |
| **Transfer** | Shift the **impact/responsibility** to a third party | Vendor support contract, managed service, insurance, SLA with penalty |
| **Accept** | Acknowledge the risk and **do nothing (or just monitor)**, usually when impact is low or cost of response exceeds benefit; document it and have a contingency | Accept risk of short outage in non-prod |

**Key takeaways**
- Risk score ≈ **likelihood × impact**. Prioritise high-score risks.
- **Mitigation reduces; it does not eliminate.** Only *avoidance* removes the risk entirely.
- **Acceptance must be a conscious, documented decision**, ideally approved by the risk owner/stakeholder.
- Every risk needs an **owner**, a **mitigation/response**, and a **review** point.
- Unvalidated **assumptions** and **constraints that conflict with requirements** are prime sources of risk.
- Design-level mitigation in VCF examples: redundancy (A), validated designs and standardised domains (M), capacity headroom (P), tested backups/DR (R), defence-in-depth (S).

## Objective 1.6: Documenting Design Decisions, Mapping to Requirements, Specifying Implications

**A good design decision record contains:**
1. **Decision ID / name**: unique and traceable
2. **Requirement(s) addressed**: link to requirement ID(s)
3. **Context / problem**: what was being decided
4. **Options considered**: alternatives evaluated
5. **Decision (justification)**: what was chosen and **why**
6. **Implications (impact)**: consequences, positive **and negative**, on AMPRS qualities, cost, operations, other decisions, and risks

**Example**

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| ID | DD-007 |
| Requirement | BR-02: critical workloads must survive host failure; TR-05: RPO = 0 within a site |
| Decision | Use vSAN ESA with FTT=2 for the production workload domain cluster |
| Justification | Meets availability requirement, ESA offers efficient erasure coding and performance |
| Implications | (+) Availability. (−) Higher raw capacity needs and cost; minimum host count increases; requires validated NVMe hardware (constraint) |

**Key takeaways**
- **Every decision must map to at least one requirement.** A decision with no requirement is unjustified (gold plating).
- **Implications are mandatory**: state trade-offs, effects on AMPRS, dependencies on other decisions, and new risks/constraints created.
- Document **alternatives considered and why rejected**; it shows the decision was reasoned.
- Maintain a **traceability matrix**: Requirement ↔ Design decision ↔ Validation test.
- Decisions should be **justified, specific, and unambiguous**; they feed design reviews, audits, and change management.
- Decisions can create **new requirements, constraints, or risks**, which should be captured.

## Objective 1.7: Design Validation Strategies

**Purpose:** prove the design **meets requirements**, respects constraints, and handles risks, *before and after* implementation.

| Strategy | When | What it checks |
|---|---|---|
| **Requirements traceability review** | Design phase | Every requirement is covered by a decision; every decision maps to a requirement |
| **Design / peer / stakeholder review** | Design phase | Correctness, completeness, assumptions, alignment with business needs |
| **Compatibility and standards check** | Design phase | Hardware/software on VMware compatibility lists; interoperability; supported versions and configurations; adherence to VMware/VCF validated patterns |
| **AMPRS assessment** | Design phase | Each quality is addressed; trade-offs are understood; SPOFs identified |
| **Capacity and performance modelling / sizing** | Design phase | Headroom, scalability, growth |
| **Proof of concept (PoC) / pilot / lab** | Pre-production | Feasibility, validates assumptions and risky technology choices |
| **Test plans (functional, failover, performance, security, DR)** | Implementation / acceptance | Failure scenarios (host/link/site), failover/failback, recovery vs RTO/RPO, load, penetration/compliance scans |
| **Acceptance / sign-off** | End of build | Delivered solution meets agreed criteria |
| **Health and compliance monitoring** | Operations | Continued conformance, drift detection |

**Key takeaways**
- Validate against **requirements**, not personal preference. Success criteria must be **measurable** and agreed up front.
- **Assumptions are validated** (via PoC, discovery, stakeholder confirmation) → they become facts or risks.
- Validation is **continuous**: design review → build verification → acceptance testing → operational monitoring.
- **Test failure scenarios**, not just the happy path: availability and recoverability claims need failover/DR tests.
- **Traceability matrix** closes the loop: Requirement → Decision → Test → Result.
- Using **VCF validated, standardised architecture** reduces risk and supports validation through known-good patterns and built-in compatibility checks.

## Quick Recall Sheet

- **Business req** = outcome (what/why). **Technical req** = capability (how, measurable).
- **Conceptual** = vision. **Logical** = components and relationships (vendor-neutral). **Physical** = exact products, sizes, IPs.
- **Requirement** = must do. **Assumption** = unverified belief. **Constraint** = limit. **Risk** = potential negative event.
- **AMPRS** = Availability, Manageability, Performance, Recoverability, Security. Every decision involves trade-offs; cost is *not* one of the five.
- **Risk responses**: Avoid, Mitigate, Transfer, Accept.
- **Decision record** = requirement + options + justification + **implications**.
- **Validation** = traceability, reviews, compatibility checks, PoC, failure/DR testing, acceptance, ongoing monitoring.
